Defining Science

Overview

This lecture explains how scientists use evidence to answer questions and build reliable knowledge.

Scientific Research

Definition

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world around us.

Science uses a careful, organized process to explain how the natural world works. The scientific method is a structured process for collecting and evaluating evidence to test ideas and answer questions.

Science is not about finding absolute truths. Knowledge becomes stronger over time as researchers collect more evidence, improve methods, and refine explanations.

Although scientific research is one of the most reliable ways of acquiring knowledge, it requires time, resources, and funding and is best suited to questions answerable through observation and measurable evidence.

Major Steps in the Scientific Method

Step Purpose
1. Make an Observation Identify a phenomenon and develop a research question.
2. Review What is Already Known Examine prior research and existing knowledge.
3. Make a Prediction Develop a testable hypothesis.
4. Select a Research Design Determine how evidence will be collected.
5. Analyze Data & Report Results Evaluate evidence and communicate findings.

Step 1: Make an Observation

The scientific method begins with observation. Every investigation starts when someone notices something interesting, unexpected, or important.

Observations may come from personal experiences, previous studies, existing data, or information shared by others. These observations help identify patterns, problems, and opportunities for inquiry.

Example: Isaac Newton

Newton observed an apple falling from a tree and asked: Why do objects fall downward instead of upward? This question contributed to theories of gravity and motion.

Definition: Population

A population is the entire group of individuals, objects, or events that a researcher wishes to study.

Example: If studying Tesla owners in the United States, the population includes all Tesla owners in the country.

Key Takeaway

Scientific research begins with curiosity. Researchers observe the world, identify a question, and seek evidence to answer it.

Step 2: Review What Is Already Known

Before conducting new research, scientists examine existing knowledge through a literature review.

Literature includes journal articles, books, scientific reports, and other credible sources relevant to a research topic.

Definition: Literature Review

A literature review is a systematic examination of existing research that summarizes current knowledge, identifies gaps, and explains how new studies contribute to a field.

A Literature Review Should

  • Summarize what is currently known.
  • Identify unanswered questions and research gaps.
  • Identify relevant theories.
  • Explain how new research contributes to knowledge.
Research in Organizations

Organizations often review internal data, employee feedback, customer insights, industry reports, expert opinions, and competitor information before making decisions.

Key Takeaway

Scientists begin by examining existing evidence to understand what is known and what questions still require investigation.

Step 3: Make a Prediction

After identifying a research question and reviewing the literature, researchers develop a hypothesis.

Definition: Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables.

Example: Exercise and Blood Pressure

People who exercise more have lower blood pressure.

Exercise and blood pressure can both be measured, making this hypothesis testable.

Variables in a Hypothesis

  • Exercise: Amount of physical activity.
  • Blood Pressure: Indicator of cardiovascular health.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Hypothesis Type Meaning Example
Null (H₀) No relationship exists. Exercise is not related to blood pressure.
Alternative (H₁) A relationship exists. More exercise is associated with lower blood pressure.
Key Takeaway

A hypothesis converts a research question into a testable prediction.

Hypotheses vs. Facts, Laws, and Theories

Term Definition Example
Facts Objective observations describing what happens. Water boils at 100°C at sea level.
Laws Describe consistent patterns in nature. Newton's Laws; Laws of Thermodynamics.
Hypotheses Testable predictions. Exercise is related to blood pressure.
Theories Evidence-based explanations for why phenomena occur. Relativity, Natural Selection, Theory of Planned Behavior.
Important Note

A scientific theory is not "just a theory." It represents one of the strongest forms of scientific explanation and is supported by extensive evidence.

Step 4: Select the Research Study Design

Researchers determine how they will collect evidence to test their hypothesis.

Definition: Sample

A sample is a smaller group selected from a population that researchers can realistically access and study.

Example: Tesla Owners

Rather than studying all Tesla owners in the United States, researchers may collect data from a sample of 100 owners.

Independent and Dependent Variables

Variable Type Definition Example
Independent Variable Variable expected to influence outcomes. Exercise level.
Dependent Variable Outcome being measured. Blood pressure.

Additional Variables

Type Definition Example
Mediator Explains how or why an effect occurs. Endorphins may explain exercise effects.
Moderator Changes strength or direction of a relationship. Type of exercise.
Confound Uncontrolled factor producing misleading conclusions. Diet may influence blood pressure.
Key Takeaway

Researchers must carefully plan study design, select samples, and identify variables before collecting data.

Step 5: Analyze the Data & Report the Results

After collecting data, researchers use statistical methods to determine whether evidence supports the hypothesis.

Definition: Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis uses mathematical methods to organize, summarize, and evaluate data.

The Importance of Sample Representation

Example: Sampling Bias

If a population is evenly divided between males and females but a sample contains 80% males and 20% females, results may be biased.

Why Sampling Matters

  • Representative samples improve accuracy.
  • Biased samples produce misleading results.
  • Larger and more diverse samples improve confidence.

Generalizability

Generalizability refers to the extent to which findings can be applied beyond the sample studied.

Questions to Consider
  • Who participated in the study?
  • Were important groups excluded?
  • How large was the sample?
  • Does the sample resemble the population?
Key Takeaway

Good research depends on analyzing evidence appropriately and collecting data from representative samples that support meaningful conclusions.
