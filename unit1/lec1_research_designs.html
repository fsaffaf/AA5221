Variables

Independent Variable: The variable manipulated by the researcher. It causes changes in the outcome variable. It is often abbreviated as IV.
Dependent Variable: The variable that is measured. It is affected by the independent variable. It is often abbreviated as DV.

Research Designs

All three research designs (true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study) are based off of different combinations of three key features.

Term Definition Example
Manipulation The researcher creates different groups or conditions. The researcher creates two groups. Group A will study for 1 hour, and Group B will study for 2 hours.
Random Assignment Participants are randomly placed into groups. A computer randomly assigns half of the students to Group A, and the other half to Group B.
Control Everything else is kept as similar as possible between groups. All students were given the same study guide, the same library, and the same materials to study. They were given the same exam in the same room with the same instructor. This was to ensure everything between the two groups was equal, except for study time.

True Experiment

True Experiment: A true experiment is the only research design that allows us to claim causality. To "claim causality" just means that we can say that the independent variable (IV) truly caused the dependent variable (DV). The other two research designs cannot make this claim. A true experiment is the only research design which has all three features. These three features allow the researcher to remove as much bias as possible from the research, so that they can conflidently say the IV truly caused the DV.

Research Question Examples

  • Is there a difference in sleep (hours) between people on a keto-diet versus a standard American diet?
  • Is there a difference in exam score (percentage) between students who studied alone versus in a group?
  • Is there a difference in running speed between those who trained with Personal Trainer A versus Personal Trainer B?

Hypothesis Format

  • Null: There is NO difference in the scores between Group A and Group B.
  • Alternate: There IS a difference in the scores between Group A and Group B.

Quasi-Experiment

Quasi-Experiment: A quasi-experiment is the second strongest reserach design. The prefix "quasi" means almost. A quasi-experiment is an "almost" experiment because it containes only one or two features required for a true experiment. A quasi-experiment does NOT allow the researcher to claim causality.

Although a quasi-experiment is weaker than a true experiment, a quasi-experiment is still an important and useful research design. In fact, there are times when a researcher will intentionally choose a quasi-experiment over a true experiment. Typically, this is becasue the researcher cannot always randomly assign participants to groups. There are many scenarios where the researcher cannot assign participants to groups due to nature or ethical reasons. For example, it is not naturally possible to randomly assign participants to be born male or female. Additionally, it is not ethically possible to randomly assign participants to have a traumatic brain injury or not.

Correlational Study

Correlational studies are the weakest design. Just because two variables are related, we cannot assume that one causes the other. There may be a third variable, or the relationship could be coincidental. Because of this, there is technically no "true" independent variable or dependent variable in a correlational study. Often times they are simply referred to as "variables." Despite being the weakest design, correlational studies are useful because they are fast, inexpensive, and great for early exploration.

Types of Experiments: Between vs Within Designs

In true experiments and quasi-experiments, researchers must compare groups. Each group experiences a different level of the independent variable, called "copnditions." When researchers compare conditions, they must decide how participants experience those conditions. There are two main approaches, and each has important trade-offs.

Between-Subjects Design

Each participant is exposed to only one condition.

In this design, different groups of participants are used for each condition. For example, one group might receive Medication A, while another group receives Medication B.

  • ✅ Faster overall study completion
  • ✅ No carryover effects between conditions
  • ❌ Requires more participants
  • ❌ Groups may differ in unintended ways

Because each group is different, researchers must work harder to ensure the groups are comparable.

Within-Subjects Design

Each participant experiences all conditions.

In this design, the same participants go through every condition. For example, each participant might take Medication A first, and then Medication B.

  • ✅ Requires fewer participants
  • ✅ Reduces variability between people
  • ❌ Takes longer to complete
  • ❌ Risk of carryover effects

A key challenge is that earlier conditions may influence later ones. Researchers often use techniques like counterbalancing to reduce this issue.

Sample vs. Population

The population is the entire group you are interested in studying. For example, if you are studying Tesla owners in the US, the population includes all Tesla owners across the country (let's say that number is 600,000).

A sample is small part of the population that you can realistically access and collect data from. For example, let's say we could only collect data from 100 Tesla owners.

Descriptive statistics focus on describing the sample (e.g., the 100 Tesla owners). They help you summarize and understand the details of your data, such as central tendencies and variability.

Inferential statistics aim to make predictions about the population (e.g., the 600,000 Tesla owners) based on the findings within your sample.

When conducting inferential statistics, the composition of your sample is critically important. The accuracy and usefulness of your conclusions depend on how well your sample represents the population you are studying.

For example, imagine your population of interest is 50% male and 50% female, but your collected sample is 80% male and 20% female. In this case, any inferences drawn from your sample may not accurately reflect the population, because the sample is biased and unrepresentative. The study of how samples are drawn and how representative they are is a branch of statistics called sampling theory. While it is a complex and deep topic, it is essential for ensuring that your inferential statistics are valid (true) and reliable (repeatable).
