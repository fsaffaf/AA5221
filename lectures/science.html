Overview

Before defining what science is, it is helpful to understand what science is not.

Science is Not Personal Feelings

Sometimes we believe something is true simply because it feels true, even when there is no evidence. Feelings are subjective and can be influenced by emotions, expectations, or personal biases. Because of this, personal feelings alone are not a reliable source of scientific knowledge. For example, you may meet a person for the first time and feel that they are untrustworthy, even though they have not done anything wrong.

Science is Not Personal Experience

Personal experience is limited and can be misleading. We can only observe a small part of the world, and our past experiences often shape how we interpret new events. This influence is called bias. Observations are naturally influenced by prior experiences, which can create biases in how we interpret events. For example, you may wear your favorite football player’s jersey while watching a game, and your team wins. After this happens once or twice, you may believe that wearing the jersey causes the team to win. In reality, this conclusion is based on coincidence, not evidence.

Science is Not Authority Figures

Authority figures include people or institutions in positions of power, such as parents, teachers, religious leaders, the media, or the government. It is common to accept information because it comes from an authority figure. Authority figures can be caring, sincere, and confident, yet still incorrect. Because of this, science does not accept claims as true simply because an authority figure says they are true. Instead, scientific claims must be supported by evidence that can be tested and evaluated. For example, sometimes parents tell children that if they swallow chewing gum, it will stay in their stomach for several years. Parents usually say this to discourage children from swallowing gum, and their intentions are good. However, this claim is not scientifically accurate. Chewing gum is not digested, but it passes through the body in a few days.

These Ways of Knowing Are Still Useful

Personal feelings, personal experience, and authority figures are not science. However, this does not mean these ways of knowing are always wrong or useless. In everyday life, these ways of knowing often help us make decisions quickly and function effectively. For example, personal experience can help you learn which study habits work best for you. You may notice that you understand material better when you study in a quiet space rather than with music playing. It does not matter that this technique may not apply to everyone.

Science is the Scientific Method

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world around us.

This means that science uses a careful, organized, and planned process to explain how the natural world works. The scientific method is a structured process for collecting and evaluating evidence to test ideas and answer questions. Science is not about finding absolute truths. Science does not (and should not) claim to have perfect or final answers. Science is about getting closer to the truth as we learn more. Over time, scientific explanations become better, more precise, and more useful. The scientific method is one of the most reliable ways to gain knowledge, but it also has limits. It can take a lot of time and resources, and it cannot answer every type of question. The scientific method is best used for questions that can be tested through observation and evidence. The scientific method is a structured process that scientists use to ask questions, test ideas, and build knowledge. Although specific techniques may differ across disciplines, the core steps of the scientific method are consistent.

The main steps of the scientific method are:

  • Make an Observation
  • Write a Literature Review
  • Form the Hypotheses
  • Select the Research Study Design
  • Analyze the Data & Draw a Conclusion

Steps

Step 1: Make an Observation

The scientific method begins with observation. Every scientific investigation starts when someone notices something interesting or unusual in the world. Observations may come from personal feelings, experience, or authority. These initial observations help scientists identify patterns or events that need explanation. Observations naturally lead to questions. Asking a clear and focused question helps guide the investigation. For example, the mathematician Isaac Newton observed an apple falling from a tree. This observation led him to ask an important question: Why do objects fall downward instead of upward?

Step 2: Write a Literature Review

Before conducting new research, scientists examine what is already known about the topic. This is called a literature review. The literature refers to the existing body of published research on a specific topic, including scholarly articles, books, and reports. A literature review is more than a summary. A literature review should:

  • Summarize known research
  • Identify questions
  • Identify theories
  • Explain contribution

Step 3: Form Hypotheses

Once a research question is clearly defined, the next step is to form a hypothesis. A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables. Scientists do not try to prove hypotheses; instead, they test them using evidence. A hypothesis is testable if it can be examined using data that can be observed, measured, and analyzed. For example, the hypothesis “People who exercise more have lower blood pressure” is testable because exercise can be measured in hours per week, and blood pressure can be measured using medical instruments.

The next step is to select the correct research study design to test the hypothesis. This step requires understanding different types of variables. A variable is any attribute that can change, such as age, income, dosage, or test scores. Researchers typically focus on the independent variable (manipulated) and dependent variable (measured). They must also consider additional variables that may influence results including mediators, moderators, and confounds.

The final step of the scientific method is conducting data analyses and interpreting the results. Researchers analyze data using statistics to determine whether the results support the null hypothesis or alternate hypothesis. The statistical test you choose depends on your research study design and the types of variables you have. Remember, scientific conclusions are always open to revision. New evidence, improved methods, or additional studies may refine or change scientific understanding.

Generalizability: Generalizability refers to the extent to which study findings can be applied beyond the specific sample studied. When evaluating research, it is important to ask: Do these results apply to other people or settings? Generalizability increases when studies use large, diverse samples. Research findings are most applicable to populations that closely resemble the study sample.Key questions to consider include: Who participated in the study? Were important demographic groups excluded? How large was the sample?

Research Designs

The three study designs are: true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study. In this class, we will start with basic research designs. Specifically, we will focus on research studies with one independent variable and one dependent variable.

True Experiment

A true experiment allows the researcher to say that the independent variable truly causes the dependent variable. The ability to claim the independent variable causes the dependent variable is known as “causality.” True Experiment: A true experiment must have all three of the following: 1) Manipulation of the independent variable, 2) Random assignment to groups, 3) High experimental control. Requirement #1: Manipulation of the Independent Variable When a researcher "manipulates" the independent variable, this means the researcher splits the independent variable into two groups: Group A and Group B. For example, if the independent variable is headache medicine, the researcher can create a high dose group (100 mg) and a low dose group (50mg). Not all variables can be manipulated by a researcher. Some variables are "fixed" or pre-determined and cannot be changed (for example, gender, nationality, age, etc). Requirement #2: Random Assignment to Groups When a researcher randomly assigns participants to the groups, this means that the researcher uses a computer to randomly decide which participants go into Group A, and which people go into Group B. This makes sure everyone has an equal chance of being selected for either group. For example, a researcher has 100 participants. In random assignment, the researcher will not pick who should be Group A or Group B. The participants also will not who should be in Group A or Group B. Instead, the computer will randomly pick 50 people to be in Group A and the other 50 people to be in Group B. Requirement #3: High Experimental Control When a researcher has high experimental control, this means that everything (except the independent variable) is the same (or as equal as possible) for Group A and Group B. Anything that cannot be made equal is measured so that researchers can use statistics. The purpose is to make sure that the only difference between the groups is the independent variable. This way, the researcher can properly determine if the independent variable is truly causing the dependent variable. In our headache research example, the researcher would make sure participants have a similar health history, are at the same hospital, given the medication at the same time of day, in the same method (pill), in the same room, by the same assistant, and so on.

Quasi

"Quasi" means "almost." A quasi-experiment is an "almost" experiment because it contains one or two out of the three ingredients of a true experiment. It can be any combination of manipulation of the independent variable, random assignment to the groups, and high experimental control. Most commonly, the quasi-experiment lacks random assignment. Because it does not have all three ingredients, this study design cannot claim that the independent variable causes the dependent variable. Quasi-Experiment: A quasi-experiment has one or two the following: 1) Manipulation of the independent variable, 2) Random assignment to groups, 3) High experimental control. Quasi-experiments are the second strongest research design. A quasi-experiment is often chosen instead of a true experiment due to ethical or practical constraints. For example, it may be unethical to randomly assign people to be exposed to illegal drugs or not. Instead, researchers must study people who are already taking illegal drugs and do not want to get treatment. Another example is perhaps the researchers do not have enough time or money to do random assignment.

Correlational

Correlational designs examine relationships between variables without any of the three ingredients of a true experiment. Because it does not have all three ingredients, this study design cannot claim that the independent variable causes the dependent variable. There is no manipulation of the independent variable, random assignment to the groups, or high experimental control. Although a correlational study is the weakest study, it is still useful. This study is very cheap and quick to conduct. It can be used to establish a relationship between variables, and provide evidence to continue with a second stronger study such as a quasi-experiment or true experiment.

Correlational Study: A correlational study must have none of the following: 1) Manipulation of the independent variable, 2) Random assignment to groups, 3) High experimental control.

Between vs Within

True Experiments and Quasi-Experiments involve comparing groups. There are two ways to design groups: between-subjects design or within-subjects design. Between-Subjects Design: Participants experience only one of the two conditions. In a between-subjects design, participants only experience one condition. For example, if a researcher wants to compare the effectiveness of two different medications for treating headaches, participants will only take one of the medications. Because each medication takes two weeks to show its true effects, participants must stay on their assigned medication for the full two-week period before their outcomes are measured. More specifically, if the researcher recruits 100 participants, 50 will take Medication A for two weeks and the other 50 will take Medication B for two weeks.The benefit of a between-subjects design is that the data for both groups can be collected during the same general time period, making it a relatively fast way to gather results—especially when treatments require time to take effect, such as the two-week period needed for each medication.However, a limitation of this design is that it requires larger sample sizes, since each condition needs its own separate group of participants. It can also be difficult to ensure that both groups are truly comparable, which may introduce confounding variables. For example, what if the 50 participants who received Medication A were on average around 60 years old, while the participants who received Medication B were around 20 years old? If one medication appears more effective, it may be unclear whether the difference is due to the medication itself or differences in the participants’ ages. Within-Subjects Design: Participants experience both of the two conditions. In a within-subjects design, participants experience both conditions. Using a medication example, imagine a researcher wants to compare the effectiveness of Medication A and Medication B for treating headaches. Because each medication takes two weeks to show its true effects, participants would take Medication A for two weeks and then Medication B for two weeks, allowing the researcher to compare how the same participants respond to each treatment.The benefit of a within-subjects design is that you can use fewer participants and still collect the same amount of data. For example, the researcher could recruit 50 participants, have all 50 take Medication A for two weeks, and then have those same 50 participants take Medication B for two weeks. In the end, the researcher still has 100 data points (50 responses for each medication) but only needed half as many participants. Requiring fewer participants often means fewer resources—such as time and money—are needed to conduct the research.However, there are also limitations. Within-subjects research often takes longer than between-subjects research. If each medication requires two weeks to show its effects, participants would need four weeks total to complete the study (two weeks for Medication A and two weeks for Medication B). In a between-subjects design, by contrast, participants would only take one medication for two weeks, so the entire study could be completed more quickly.Another issue is the possibility of carryover effects, meaning the experience in the first condition might influence results in the second condition. For example, if participants take Medication A for two weeks and then switch to Medication B for two weeks, the effects of Medication A might still influence how participants respond to Medication B.That is one reason some researchers choose a between-subjects design instead. In that case, 50 participants might take Medication A for two weeks, while another 50 participants take Medication B for two weeks at the same time. This approach shortens the overall study time and prevents carryover effects between treatments.Both research designs have advantages and disadvantages. The researcher’s choice to use a between-subjects or within-subjects design depends on the goals of the study, as well as practical considerations like time, cost, and available participants.
