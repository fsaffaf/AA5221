Scientific Research

Overview

This lecture explains how scientists use evidence to answer questions and build reliable knowledge.

Scientific Research

Definition

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world around us.

Science uses a careful, organized, and planned process to explain how the natural world works. The scientific method is a structured process for collecting and evaluating evidence to test ideas and answer questions.

Science is not about finding absolute truths. Instead, scientific knowledge becomes stronger over time as researchers collect more evidence, improve their methods, and refine existing explanations.

Although the scientific method is one of the most reliable ways of acquiring knowledge, it also has limitations. Scientific research can require significant time, resources, and funding, and it is best suited for questions that can be answered using observation and measurable evidence.

While specific research techniques differ across disciplines, the overall scientific process remains remarkably consistent. The major steps are summarized below.

Step Purpose
1. Make an Observation Identify an interesting phenomenon and ask a research question.
2. Review What is Already Known Examine previous research to understand what has already been discovered.
3. Make a Prediction Develop a hypothesis that can be tested with evidence.
4. Select a Research Design Decide how data will be collected to test the hypothesis.
5. Analyze Data & Report Results Evaluate evidence and determine whether findings support the hypothesis.

Step 1: Make an Observation

The scientific method begins with observation. Every scientific investigation starts when someone notices something interesting, unexpected, or important in the world.

Observations may come from personal experiences, previous research, existing data, or information shared by others.

Observations naturally lead to questions. Asking a clear and focused research question helps guide the investigation and determines what information needs to be collected.

Example: Isaac Newton

The mathematician Isaac Newton observed an apple falling from a tree. This simple observation led him to ask an important scientific question: Why do objects fall downward instead of upward? This question contributed to the development of theories about gravity and motion.

In organizations and companies, observations often come from problems identified by employees, customers, investors, or company leaders.

Definition: Population

A population is the entire group of individuals, objects, or events that a researcher is interested in studying.

For example, if you are studying Tesla owners in the United States, the population would include all Tesla owners in the country.

Key Takeaway

Scientific research begins with curiosity. Researchers first observe the world, identify something they want to understand, and develop questions that can be answered using evidence.

Step 2: Review What Is Already Known

Before conducting new research, scientists first examine what is already known about the topic. This process is called a literature review.

The literature refers to the existing body of knowledge on a specific topic.

Definition: Literature Review

A literature review is a systematic examination of existing research on a specific topic. It summarizes what is currently known, identifies unanswered questions, and explains how a new study contributes to the field.

A Literature Review Should:

  • Summarize what is currently known.
  • Identify unanswered questions or gaps.
  • Identify relevant theories.
  • Explain how a study contributes new knowledge.
Research in Organizations

Organizations often review expert opinions, employee feedback, customer feedback, internal company data, industry reports, and competitor information before making important decisions.

Key Takeaway

Scientists do not begin research by guessing. They first examine existing evidence and identify where additional knowledge is needed.

Step 3: Make a Prediction

After researchers develop a research question and review what is already known, they make a prediction. In science, this prediction is called a hypothesis.

Definition: Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables.

Example: Exercise and Blood Pressure

People who exercise more have lower blood pressure.

Exercise and blood pressure can both be measured, making this hypothesis testable.

Variables in a Hypothesis

  • Exercise: Amount of physical activity.
  • Blood Pressure: Measure of cardiovascular health.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

Hypothesis Type Meaning Example
Null Hypothesis (H₀) No relationship exists. Exercise is not related to blood pressure.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁) A relationship exists. People who exercise more have lower blood pressure.
Key Takeaway

A hypothesis transforms a research question into a testable prediction.

Hypotheses vs. Facts, Laws, and Theories

These scientific terms represent different forms of knowledge and serve different purposes.

Term Definition Example
Facts Objective observations about the world. Water boils at 100°C at sea level.
Laws Describe what happens in a consistent manner. Newton's Laws of Motion.
Hypotheses Testable predictions. Exercise affects blood pressure.
Theories Evidence-based explanations for why phenomena occur. Theory of Relativity, Theory of Planned Behavior, Natural Selection.
Important Note

A scientific theory is not "just a theory." It is one of the strongest forms of scientific explanation because it is supported by substantial evidence from many studies.

Step 4: Select the Research Study Design

After developing a hypothesis, researchers must determine how they will collect evidence.

Definition: Sample

A sample is a smaller group selected from a population that researchers can realistically access and collect data from.

Independent and Dependent Variables

Variable Type Definition Example
Independent Variable (IV) The variable expected to influence an outcome. Exercise
Dependent Variable (DV) The outcome being measured. Blood Pressure

Additional Variables to Consider

Variable Type Purpose Example
Mediator Explains how or why an effect occurs. Endorphin release.
Moderator Changes the strength of a relationship. Type of exercise.
Confound Uncontrolled variable affecting results. Diet.
Key Takeaway

Researchers must carefully select a sample and identify the variables needed to test their hypotheses accurately.

Step 5: Analyze the Data & Report the Results

The final step of the scientific method is analyzing data and interpreting results.

Definition: Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis is the process of organizing, summarizing, and evaluating data in order to answer research questions.

The Importance of Sample Representation

Example: Sampling Bias

If a population is 50% male and 50% female but the sample is 80% male and 20% female, conclusions may not accurately represent the population.

Sampling Theory

Sampling theory examines how samples are selected and how accurately they represent populations.

Why Sampling Matters
  • Representative samples improve accuracy.
  • Biased samples can produce misleading results.
  • Larger and more diverse samples often increase confidence in findings.

Generalizability

Generalizability refers to the extent to which findings can be applied beyond the original sample.

Questions to Consider
  • Who participated in the study?
  • Were important groups excluded?
  • How large was the sample?
  • Does the sample resemble the population?
Key Takeaway

Good research depends not only on statistical analysis but also on collecting data from appropriate and representative samples.
