Descriptive Statistics

Overview

Statistics can be divided into two major categories: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics. This lecture focuses on descriptive statistics. The next lecture will discuss inferential statistics, which are used to test hypotheses.

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive Statistics

Brief quantitative summaries that describe, show, or summarize the features of a specific dataset. They describe what is happening in the data without making predictions or drawing conclusions about a larger population.

Data Visualization

This section focuses on how we can visually inspect individual variables using bar graphs or histograms. This means we are looking at one variable at a time.

The purpose of descriptive statistics is to describe the data and understand the type and distribution of the variables. It is not to draw conclusions about hypotheses. Testing hypotheses requires inferential statistics.

Understanding the type of data you have helps determine how you can visualize it.

Bar Graph

For a single variable, a bar graph displays categorical data. It shows how many times each category occurs in a dataset.

Used for: Categorical data, including nominal and ordinal variables.
Purpose: To compare the frequency or value of separate categories.
Bars: Each bar represents a separate category. Bars do not touch because the categories are separate.
X-axis: Categories
Y-axis: Frequency or value
Histogram

For a single variable, a histogram displays numerical data. Because numerical data can contain many possible values along a scale, a histogram groups values into ranges called bins.

The histogram shows how many observations fall within each bin.

Used for: Continuous data, including interval and ratio data.
Purpose: To examine the distribution and normality of numerical data.
Bars: Bars touch because the values represent continuous ranges.
X-axis: Ranges of values (bins)
Y-axis: Frequency

Normality

Normality

Normality describes whether continuous data follow an approximately symmetrical, bell-shaped distribution. Understanding the distribution of the data helps determine which inferential statistical tests may be appropriate. Normality only applies to continuous data. Categorical variables, including nominal and ordinal data, do not have a normal distribution.

Key characteristics of an approximately normal distribution:

  1. Mean, median, and mode are similar: The three measures of central tendency occur near the center of the distribution.
  2. Symmetry: The left and right sides of the distribution are approximately mirror images.
  3. Bell-shaped curve: Most observations occur near the center, with fewer observations occurring at extreme values.
The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule)

The empirical rule describes how observations are distributed in an approximately normal distribution. It allows researchers to estimate where most observations fall without examining every individual score.

68%: Approximately 68% of observations fall within ±1 standard deviation of the mean.

95%: Approximately 95% of observations fall within ±2 standard deviations of the mean.

99.7%: Approximately 99.7% of observations fall within ±3 standard deviations of the mean.

Standard Deviation

Standard deviation (SD) measures how much individual observations differ from the mean. A variable with a low SD indicates participants generally scored close to the mean. A variable with a high SD indicates participants scores varied greatly from the mean. This indicates the mean is not an accurate representaiton of how most people scored. It also may indicate the data is abnormally distributed.
In a normal distribution, standard deviation can be used to determine how much of the data falls within specific ranges.

Skewness

Skewness describes the degree to which a distribution is not symmetrical.

Positive Skewness: The tail extends toward the right side of the distribution. Most observations are concentrated on the left side, with a few observations having unusually high values.

Negative Skewness: The tail extends toward the left side of the distribution. Most observations are concentrated on the right side, with a few observations having unusually low values.

Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the shape of a distribution, particularly the degree of peakedness and the thickness of the tails.

Kurtosis values are calculated automatically by statistical software.

Leptokurtic: The distribution is taller and narrower, with values more concentrated around the center.
Mesokurtic: The distribution is approximately normal.
Platykurtic: The distribution is wider and flatter, with values more spread out across the range.
