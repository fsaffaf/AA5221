Descriptive Statistics

Statistics can be divided into two major categories: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics.

Descriptive Statistics

  • Describe the characteristics of a sample.
  • Summarize and organize data.
  • Help determine which inferential tests should be used.

Inferential Statistics

Used to test hypotheses and make conclusions about populations based on sample data.

Numerical Descriptive Statistics

Statistic Definition Appropriate Data
Mean Mathematical average. Normally distributed continuous data.
Median Middle value in an ordered dataset. Ordinal data or non-normal continuous data.
Mode Most frequently occurring value. Most commonly used for categorical data.
Standard Deviation Measures variability around the mean. Normally distributed continuous data.
Shapiro-Wilk Test Statistical test used to assess normality. Continuous data.
Standard Deviation

Standard deviation (SD) measures how much individual observations differ from the mean.

  • Low SD: Values are closely clustered around the mean.
  • High SD: Values are widely spread around the mean.

In a normal distribution, standard deviation can be used to determine how much of the data falls within specific ranges.

The Empirical Rule (68-95-99.7 Rule)

The empirical rule describes how observations are distributed in an approximately normal distribution.

  • 68%: Approximately 68% of observations fall within ±1 standard deviation of the mean.
  • 95%: Approximately 95% of observations fall within ±2 standard deviations of the mean.
  • 99.7%: Approximately 99.7% of observations fall within ±3 standard deviations of the mean.
The empirical rule allows researchers to estimate where most observations fall without examining every individual score.

Standard deviation can also be used to determine how far an individual score is from the mean and allows researchers to compare scores across different distributions.

Data Visualization

How to Visually Inspect Individual Variables

This lecture focuses on how we can visually inspect individual variables using bar charts or histograms. To be clear, this means we are looking at ONE variable at a time. The purpose of descriptive statistics is to determine the type of data we have, not to draw conclusions about our hypotheses (which would require inspecting at least TWO variables in one chart. Understanding the type of data you have determines how you can visualize it.

Graph Purpose Data Type
Bar Graph For a single variable, a bar graph displays categorical variables. It shows how many times each category of that variable occured in a dataset. Each bar represents a category, and the height of the bar represents the number of observations (typically, this is the number of participants). Categorical Data
Histogram For a single variable, this is a graph that displays numerical data. Because numerical data has many points along a scale, the histgram must create it's own bars to visualize the data. Histograms group values along the scale into ranges called "bins." The histogram show how many observations fall within each bin. Continuous Data

Checking Normality

Inferential statistics are used to determine whether evidence supports the null or alternative hypothesis. The inferential test selected depends on whether continuous variables are normally distributed.

Failing to examine data distribution can lead to incorrect statistical test selection and inaccurate conclusions.

Normality only applies to continuous data. Categorical variables (nominal and ordinal data) do not have a normal distribution.

Normal Distribution

A normal distribution occurs when continuous data follow a predictable mathematical pattern.

Key characteristics of an approximately normal distribution:

  1. Mean, median, and mode are similar: The three measures of central tendency occur near the center of the distribution.
  2. Symmetry: The left and right sides of the distribution are approximately mirror images.
  3. Bell-shaped curve: Most observations cluster near the center, with fewer observations occurring at extreme values.

Skewness

A distribution is symmetrical when each side is a mirror image of the other. When data are not symmetrical, the distribution is considered skewed.

Positive Skewness (Right Skew)

  • The tail extends toward the right side of the distribution.
  • Most observations are concentrated on the left side.

Negative Skewness (Left Skew)

  • The tail extends toward the left side of the distribution.
  • Most observations are concentrated on the right side.

Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the shape of the distribution curve, particularly the height and width of the curve. Kurtosis values are calculated automatically by statistical software.

  • Leptokurtic: The distribution is too peaked, meaning values are highly concentrated around the center.
  • Mesokurtic: The distribution is approximately normal.
  • Platykurtic: The distribution is too flat, meaning values are more spread out across the range.
