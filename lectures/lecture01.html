Overview

Before defining what science is, it is helpful to understand what science is not.

Science Is Not Personal Feelings

Sometimes we believe something is true simply because it feels true, even when there is no evidence. Feelings are subjective and influenced by emotion, expectations, or personal bias. Because of this, personal feelings alone are not a reliable source of scientific knowledge.

For example, you may meet a person for the first time and feel that they are untrustworthy, even though they have not done anything wrong.

Science Is Not Personal Experience

Personal experience is limited and often misleading. Our observations are shaped by prior experiences, which introduces bias.

For example, wearing your favorite football player’s jersey during a win may lead you to believe the jersey caused the victory. This is coincidence, not evidence.

Science Is Not Authority Figures

Authority figures can be sincere, knowledgeable, and confident — yet still incorrect. Science does not accept claims simply because they come from an authority.

Example: Parents may claim swallowed gum stays in your stomach for years. This is incorrect — gum passes through the digestive system in a few days.

These Ways of Knowing Are Still Useful

Personal feelings, experiences, and authority figures often help us function efficiently in everyday life. However, they are not substitutes for scientific evidence.

Science Is the Scientific Method

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world using careful, organized, and testable methods.

Science does not seek absolute truth — it aims to get closer to the truth as evidence accumulates. Scientific explanations evolve over time.

The Main Steps of the Scientific Method

  • Make an Observation
  • Write a Literature Review
  • Form the Hypotheses
  • Select the Research Study Design
  • Analyze the Data & Draw a Conclusion

Key Scientific Terms

Term Definition Example
Facts Objective observations about what happens. Water boils at 100°C at sea level.
Laws Describe how something happens, not why. Newton’s Laws of Motion
Hypotheses Testable predictions about relationships. Exercise reduces blood pressure.
Theories Well‑supported explanations of how and why. Theory of Natural Selection

Research Study Designs

The three primary designs are:

  • True Experiment
  • Quasi‑Experiment
  • Correlational Study

True Experiment

Requires manipulation, random assignment, and high experimental control. This design allows claims of causality.

Quasi‑Experiment

Lacks one or more elements of a true experiment — often random assignment. Cannot claim causality.

Correlational Study

Examines relationships without manipulation, random assignment, or control. Useful for generating hypotheses.

How to Write Hypotheses

Null Hypothesis: Nothing happens (NO relationship or difference).

Alternate Hypothesis: Something happens (IS a relationship or difference).

Design Null Hypothesis Alternate Hypothesis
Correlational No relationship exists. A relationship exists.
Experimental No group difference. A group difference exists.
