Overview

Before defining what science is, it is helpful to understand what science is not.

Science is Not Personal Feelings

Sometimes we believe something is true simply because it feels true, even when there is no evidence. Feelings are subjective and can be influenced by emotions, expectations, or personal biases. Because of this, personal feelings alone are not a reliable source of scientific knowledge.

For example, you may meet a person for the first time and feel that they are untrustworthy, even though they have not done anything wrong.

Science is Not Personal Experience

Personal experience is limited and can be misleading. We can only observe a small part of the world, and our past experiences often shape how we interpret new events. This influence is called bias.

For example, wearing your favorite football player’s jersey while watching a game and then believing it caused the team to win is coincidence, not evidence.

Science is Not Authority Figures

Authority figures can be sincere, confident, and caring—but still wrong. Science does not accept claims simply because they come from authority.

For example, parents often say swallowed gum stays in the stomach for years. This is incorrect. Gum passes through the body in a few days.

These Ways of Knowing Are Still Useful

These ways of knowing help us function efficiently in daily life, even though they are not science.

Science is the Scientific Method

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world. It uses a careful, organized, and planned process to explain how the natural world works.

Science does not claim absolute truth. Scientific explanations evolve as evidence improves.

Main Steps of the Scientific Method

  • Make an Observation
  • Write a Literature Review
  • Form the Hypotheses
  • Select the Research Study Design
  • Analyze the Data & Draw a Conclusion

Steps of the Scientific Method

Step 1: Make an Observation

The scientific method begins with observation. Observations may come from feelings, experience, or authority.

Example: Isaac Newton observing an apple fall.

Step 2: Write a Literature Review

  • Summarize current knowledge
  • Identify unanswered questions
  • Identify theories
  • Explain how new research contributes

Step 3: Form the Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about relationships between variables.

Research Study Designs

True Experiment

Requires manipulation, random assignment, and experimental control.

Quasi‑Experiment

Lacks one or more components of a true experiment, usually random assignment.

Correlational Study

Examines relationships without manipulation or control.
