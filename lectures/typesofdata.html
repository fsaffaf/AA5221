Types of Data

Overview

Understanding levels of measurement is one of the most important skills in statistics and analytics. The level of measurement determines which statistical techniques, charts, and analyses are appropriate for your data.

Why Levels of Measurement Matter

There are four types of data, also known as levels of measurement. Correctly identifying the level of measurement for your variables is essential because it determines:

  • Which descriptive statistics can be calculated
  • Which inferential statistical tests can be performed
  • Which graphs are appropriate for visualizing the data
  • How results should be interpreted and reported

Levels of Measurement

Continuous and Categorical Data

Continuous (Quantitative) Data

Numeric data that can be used for mathematical and statistical calculations.

Categorical (Qualitative) Data

Data that classify observations into categories, groups, or characteristics.

Level Definition Examples Data Type
Nominal Categories with no rank order. Race, gender, nationality Categorical
Ordinal Categories can be ranked, but the distance between categories is unknown. Single-item Likert scales Categorical
Interval Equal spacing between values, but no true zero. Temperature (°F, °C), composite Likert scales Continuous
Ratio Equal spacing between values and a meaningful zero point. Weight, height, age, income, distance Continuous

Single-Item Measures

A single-item measure uses one question to assess a variable.

Overall, how satisfied are you with your job?

Advantages: Simple, efficient, quick to administer, and reduces survey fatigue.

Limitation: A single question may not fully capture a complex concept.

Interval

Multiple-Item Measures

A multiple-item measure uses several related questions to assess the same construct. Researchers typically combine responses by calculating either a sum score or an average score.

  1. I enjoy the work I do.
  2. I am satisfied with my current job.
  3. I would recommend my workplace to others.
  4. My job meets my expectations.
Composite Measure

When several items are combined into a single score, researchers often treat the resulting measure as interval-level data, allowing more advanced statistical analyses.

Why Researchers Prefer Multiple-Item Measures

  • Greater reliability
  • Improved measurement precision
  • Reduced random error
  • Ability to evaluate measurement quality
  • More stable and consistent results
