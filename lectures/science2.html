Scientific Research

Overview

This lecture explains how scientists use evidence to answer questions and build reliable knowledge.

Scientific Research

Definition

Science is a systematic way of understanding the world around us.

Science uses a careful, organized, and planned process to explain how the natural world works. The scientific method is a structured process for collecting and evaluating evidence to test ideas and answer questions.

Science is not about finding absolute truths. Instead, scientific knowledge becomes stronger over time as researchers collect more evidence, improve their methods, and refine existing explanations.

Although the scientific method is one of the most reliable ways of acquiring knowledge, it also has limitations. Scientific research can require significant time, resources, and funding, and it is best suited for questions that can be answered using observation and measurable evidence.

While specific research techniques differ across disciplines, the overall scientific process remains remarkably consistent. The five major steps are summarized below.

Step Purpose
1. Make an Observation Identify an interesting phenomenon and ask a research question.
2. Review What is Already Known Examine previous research to understand what has already been discovered.
3. Make a Prediction Develop a hypothesis that can be tested with evidence.
4. Select a Research Design Decide how data will be collected to test the hypothesis.

Step 1: Make an Observation

The scientific method begins with observation. Every scientific investigation starts when someone notices something interesting, unexpected, or important in the world.

Observations may come from personal experiences, previous research, existing data, or information shared by others. These observations help scientists identify patterns, problems, or events that require further investigation.

Observations naturally lead to questions. Asking a clear and focused research question helps guide the investigation and determines what information needs to be collected.

Example: Isaac Newton

The mathematician Isaac Newton observed an apple falling from a tree. This simple observation led him to ask an important scientific question: Why do objects fall downward instead of upward? This question contributed to the development of theories about gravity and motion.

In organizations and companies, observations often come from problems identified by employees, customers, investors, or company leaders. These observations can highlight areas where research and data analysis may help improve decision-making.

Definition: Population

A population is the entire group of individuals, objects, or events that a researcher is interested in studying.

For example, if you are studying Tesla owners in the United States, the population would include all Tesla owners in the country. If there are approximately 600,000 Tesla owners, then the population size would be 600,000.

Key Takeaway

Scientific research begins with curiosity. Researchers first observe the world, identify something they want to understand, and develop questions that can be answered using evidence.

Step 2: Review What Is Already Known

Before conducting new research, scientists first examine what is already known about the topic. This process is called a literature review.

The literature refers to the existing body of knowledge on a specific topic. It includes previously published research, such as scholarly journal articles, books, scientific reports, and other credible sources of information.

A literature review helps researchers understand what previous studies have discovered, identify gaps in existing knowledge, and determine how their research can contribute new information.

Definition: Literature Review

A literature review is a systematic examination of existing research on a specific topic. It summarizes what is currently known, identifies unanswered questions, and explains how a new study contributes to the field.

A literature review is more than simply summarizing previous studies. A strong literature review helps researchers understand the current state of knowledge and provides a foundation for developing new research questions and hypotheses.

A Literature Review Should:

  • Summarize what is currently known about the topic.
  • Identify unanswered questions or gaps in existing research.
  • Identify relevant theories that help explain the topic.
  • Explain how the current study contributes new knowledge or improves understanding of the topic.
Research in Organizations

In organizational settings, reviewing what is already known may include more than academic research. Organizations may examine expert opinions, employee and customer feedback, internal company data, industry reports, and competitor information. These sources help organizations better understand problems before making decisions.

Key Takeaway

Scientists do not begin research by guessing. They first examine existing evidence to understand what is already known, what questions remain unanswered, and how their research can add meaningful information.

Step 3: Make a Prediction

After researchers develop a clear research question and review what is already known, the next step is to make a prediction. In science, this prediction is called a hypothesis.

A hypothesis is a specific, testable prediction about the relationship between variables. It describes what researchers expect to happen and provides a statement that can be evaluated using data.

Definition: Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable prediction about the relationship between variables. Scientists evaluate hypotheses by collecting evidence and determining whether the data support or do not support the prediction.

Scientists do not try to prove hypotheses. Instead, they test hypotheses using evidence. A hypothesis is considered testable when the variables can be observed, measured, and analyzed.

Example: Exercise and Blood Pressure

Consider the hypothesis:

"People who exercise more have lower blood pressure."

This hypothesis can be tested because exercise can be measured (for example, hours of exercise per week), and blood pressure can be measured using medical instruments. Researchers can then analyze whether the data support the predicted relationship.

Variables in a Hypothesis

Hypotheses typically describe relationships between variables. A variable is any characteristic or measurement that can take on different values.

In the example above, the variables are:

  • Exercise: the amount of physical activity a person completes.
  • Blood pressure: the measurement used to evaluate cardiovascular health.

Null and Alternative Hypotheses

When researchers test hypotheses statistically, they typically compare two competing explanations: the null hypothesis and the alternative hypothesis.

Hypothesis Type Meaning Example
Null Hypothesis (H₀) Predicts that there is no relationship or difference between variables. Exercise is not related to blood pressure.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁) Predicts that there is a relationship or difference between variables. People who exercise more have lower blood pressure.
Key Takeaway

A hypothesis transforms a research question into a testable prediction. By clearly defining expected relationships between variables, researchers can collect evidence and determine whether their predictions are supported.

Step 5: Analyze the Data & Report the Results

The final step of the scientific method is analyzing data and interpreting the results. After researchers collect information, they use statistical methods to determine whether the evidence supports or does not support their hypothesis.

The statistical analysis researchers choose depends on the research design and the types of variables collected. Different questions require different statistical approaches. For example, researchers may compare groups, examine relationships between variables, or describe patterns within the data.

Definition: Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis is the process of using mathematical methods to organize, summarize, and evaluate data in order to answer research questions.

Researchers use statistical results to evaluate whether their findings provide evidence supporting the null hypothesis or alternative hypothesis. However, scientific conclusions are always open to revision.

New evidence, improved methods, or additional studies may refine, expand, or change scientific understanding over time.

The Importance of Sample Representation

When interpreting research results, the characteristics of the sample are critically important. The accuracy and usefulness of conclusions depend on how well the sample represents the population being studied.

Example: Sampling Bias

Imagine your population of interest is 50% male and 50% female. However, the sample you collect is 80% male and 20% female.

Because the sample does not accurately represent the population, conclusions drawn from the study may not apply to everyone in the population. The sample may produce biased results.

Sampling Theory

The study of how samples are selected and how representative they are is called sampling theory.

Sampling theory is an important area of statistics because researchers often use samples to make conclusions about larger populations. If a sample is not representative, statistical conclusions may not accurately describe the population.

Why Sampling Matters
  • Representative samples improve the accuracy of conclusions.
  • Biased samples can produce misleading results.
  • Larger and more diverse samples often increase confidence that findings apply beyond the original study participants.

Generalizability

Generalizability refers to the extent to which research findings can be applied beyond the specific sample studied.

When evaluating research, researchers should ask:

Questions to Consider
  • Who participated in the study?
  • Were important demographic groups excluded?
  • How large was the sample?
  • Does the sample resemble the population we want to understand?

Research findings are most likely to generalize when studies use large, diverse samples that closely represent the population of interest.

Key Takeaway

Scientific research does not end when data are collected. Researchers must analyze evidence, interpret results, and evaluate whether findings can be generalized beyond the original sample. Good research depends not only on statistical analysis, but also on collecting data from appropriate and representative samples.
