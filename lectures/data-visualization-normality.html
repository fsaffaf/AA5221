Visual Descriptive Statistics

Understanding the type of data you have determines how you can visualize it. Bar graphs and histograms are used for different purposes.

Graph Purpose Data Type
Bar Graph A graph that displays how many times each category occurs in a dataset. Each bar represents a category, and the height of the bar represents the frequency or count of observations. Categorical Data
Histogram A graph that displays the distribution of numerical data. Histograms group values into ranges called bins and show how many observations fall within each range. Continuous Data

Bar Graphs

  • Used for categorical data (nominal and ordinal).
  • Used to compare values between groups.
  • Each bar represents a separate category.
  • Bars do not touch because categories are separate.
  • X-axis: Categories
  • Y-axis: Frequency or value

Bar charts are only needed when there are three or more groups.
The purpose of a bar chart is to make differences between multiple groups easier to see visually. When there are only two groups, the difference can usually be clearly described in a sentence, so a bar chart is unnecessary.

Publishing limitations: Scientific journals often have strict limits on the number of pages, figures, and tables that can be included in an article. Because figures take up valuable space, scientists may leave out a graph when the same information can be communicated clearly in a sentence or table. This allows them to use the available space for results that are more important or difficult to explain in text.

Histograms

  • Used for continuous data (interval and ratio).
  • Used to examine the distribution and normality of data.
  • Should be created for continuous variables.
  • Bars touch because values represent continuous ranges.
  • X-axis: Ranges of values (bins)
  • Y-axis: Frequency

Steps to Determine Which Visual Graphs to Use

  1. Identify the IV and DV in the research question.
  2. For the IV, determine if each variable is categorical (nominal or ordinal) or continuous (interval or ratio).
  3. If the IV is categorical, determine how many groups there are.
    1. If there are only 2 groups, no bar chart is needed.
    2. If there are 3 or more groups, use a bar chart.
  4. If the IV is continuous, ALWAYS use a histogram.
  5. Repeat steps 2 through 4 to determine what graph should be used to display the DV.

Checking Normality

Inferential statistics are used to determine whether evidence supports the null or alternative hypothesis. The inferential test selected depends on whether continuous variables are normally distributed.

Failing to examine data distribution can lead to incorrect statistical test selection and inaccurate conclusions.

Important:
Normality only applies to continuous data. Categorical variables (nominal and ordinal data) do not have a normal distribution.

Normal Distribution

A normal distribution occurs when continuous data follow a predictable mathematical pattern.

Key characteristics of an approximately normal distribution:

  1. Mean, median, and mode are similar: The three measures of central tendency occur near the center of the distribution.
  2. Symmetry: The left and right sides of the distribution are approximately mirror images.
  3. Bell-shaped curve: Most observations cluster near the center, with fewer observations occurring at extreme values.

Skewness

A distribution is symmetrical when each side is a mirror image of the other. When data are not symmetrical, the distribution is considered skewed.

Positive Skewness (Right Skew)

  • The tail extends toward the right side of the distribution.
  • Most observations are concentrated on the left side.

Negative Skewness (Left Skew)

  • The tail extends toward the left side of the distribution.
  • Most observations are concentrated on the right side.

Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the shape of the distribution curve, particularly the height and width of the curve. Kurtosis values are calculated automatically by statistical software.

  • Leptokurtic: The distribution is too peaked, meaning values are highly concentrated around the center.
  • Mesokurtic: The distribution is approximately normal.
  • Platykurtic: The distribution is too flat, meaning values are more spread out across the range.
