Research Design

Overview

This lecture introduces the three primary research designs used in scientific research and explains how researchers select the most appropriate design to answer a research question. Before researchers can choose a research design, they must first identify the variables they want to study. A research design is built around the relationship between variables and determines how evidence will be collected to answer a research question.

Primary Variables

Most research studies examine the relationship between two primary variables: an independent variable and a dependent variable. The independent variable is the variable that predicts or influences the outcome. The dependent variable is the outcome being measured.

Independent Variable

The independent variable is the variable that is manipulated or used to predict changes in another variable. In the hypothesis "As cybersecurity training increases, employees' ability to identify phishing emails increases," cybersecurity training is the independent variable.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the outcome that is measured to determine whether it changes. In the hypothesis "As cybersecurity training increases, employees' ability to identify phishing emails increases," phishing identification accuracy is the dependent variable because it is the outcome being measured.

Additional Variables

More advanced studies may include additional variables that help explain, strengthen, or complicate relationships between the independent and dependent variables.


Mediator

A mediator explains how or why the independent variable affects the dependent variable. For example, cybersecurity training may increase employees' knowledge of phishing warning signs, which then improves their ability to identify phishing emails.


Moderator

A moderator changes the strength or direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variable. For example, the relationship between cybersecurity training and phishing identification may be stronger for employees who regularly use email for their jobs than for employees who rarely use email.


Confounding Variable

A confounding variable is an unmeasured variable that provides an alternative explanation for the results. For example, if researchers do not measure employees' previous cybersecurity experience, it may influence their ability to identify phishing emails independently of the training. Because previous experience is not measured, researchers will not know whether differences in phishing identification are related to the training or employees' existing cybersecurity knowledge.

Research Study Designs & Key Features

Now that we understand variables, we can begin selecting the appropriate research design. Different research questions require different study designs, and each design provides different types of evidence. A research study design is the structured plan researchers use to collect evidence, measure variables, and answer a scientific research question.

There are three main research designs:true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study.

The three research designs are primarily distinguished by three scientific features: manipulation, random assignment, and control.

Manipulation

Manipulation means that the researcher created different groups for the research. For example, the researcher created a group of employees that would receive a leadership communication workshop and a group that would not receive the workshop.

Random Assignment

Random assignment means that a computer was used to randomly place participants into the different groups. For example, there were 100 employees and the computer randomly selected 50 to be in the leadership training group and the other 50 to be in the no-training group.

Control

Control means that researchers keep other factors as similar as possible so that differences in the outcome can be attributed to the independent variable. For example, employees in both groups worked in the same organization, completed the same communication assessment, and were given the same amount of time to complete it so that differences in communication scores could be attributed to the leadership workshop.

True Experiment

True Experiment

A research design with three key features: manipulation, random assignment, and control. Together, these features reduce alternative explanations and increase confidence that the independent variable caused the observed outcome. This means researchers can conclude that changes in the independent variable caused changes in the dependent variable.

Strengths
  • Researchers can examine whether one variable causes changes in another variable.
  • Random assignment helps create similar groups before the experiment begins.
  • Researchers can control other factors that could influence the results.
  • The results can provide strong evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship.
 Limitations
  • Experiments may not always reflect what happens in real-world settings.
  • Some variables cannot be manipulated because doing so would be unethical or impractical.
  • Experiments can require significant time, resources, and planning.
  • Participants may behave differently because they know they are part of a study.

Example Research Scenario

Research Question: Does cybersecurity training improve employees' ability to identify phishing emails?
Null Hypothesis: There is no difference in phishing identification scores between employees who receive cybersecurity training and employees who do not receive the training.
Alternate Hypothesis: There is a difference in phishing identification scores between employees who receive cybersecurity training and employees who do not receive the training.
Independent Variable: Cybersecurity training (training or no training)
Dependent Variable: Phishing identification score (percentage of phishing emails correctly identified)
Manipulation: The researcher provides one group of employees with a cybersecurity training session and does not provide the other group with the training.
Random Assignment: A computer randomly assigns employees to either the training group or the no-training group.
Control: All employees receive the same phishing emails, testing instructions, testing environment, and amount of time to complete the test.

Quasi-Experiment

Quasi-Experiment

A quasi-experiment is a research design that includes manipulation of the independent variable but does not include random assignment. The word quasi means "almost." A quasi-experiment is an "almost experiment" because it contains some, but not all, of the features required for a true experiment. Researchers may manipulate an independent variable, but participants cannot always be randomly assigned to groups. Because the groups are not randomly created, researchers cannot completely rule out pre-existing differences between groups.

Why Use a Quasi-Experiment?

  • Practical limitations: Some groups already exist naturally, making random assignment impossible.
  • Ethical limitations: Researchers cannot ethically assign participants to harmful or permanent conditions.
  • Real-world research: Many applied research questions involve naturally occurring groups that cannot be experimentally created.

Examples

Is there a difference in sleep hours between mothers and fathers?

Participants cannot be randomly assigned to become mothers or fathers. Is there a difference in reading level between students attending School A and School B?

Students are already enrolled and cannot be randomly assigned to schools.

Is there a difference in health outcomes between people who exercise regularly and people who do not?

Exercise habits occur naturally and cannot be randomly assigned long term.

Key Features

  • Manipulation: Yes
  • Random Assignment: No
  • Control: Often yes

Hypothesis Format

Null Hypothesis (H₀): There is no difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): There is a difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Correlational Study

Correlational Study

A correlational study is a research design used to measure the relationship between two or more variables without manipulating those variables. Correlational studies can identify whether variables are related, but they do not allow researchers to determine cause and effect.

Possible Explanations for a Correlation

When two variables are related, several explanations are possible.

  • Variable 1 causes Variable 2: One variable may influence changes in the other.
  • Variable 2 causes Variable 1: The direction of the relationship may be opposite of what researchers expected.
  • Third-variable explanation: A separate variable may explain why the two original variables are related.
  • Spurious relationship: The relationship may occur by coincidence and may not represent a meaningful connection.

Why Use Correlational Studies?

  • Exploration: Identify patterns and relationships that may be investigated in future research.
  • Prediction: Use relationships between variables to predict outcomes.
  • Practical research: Correlational studies can be faster, less expensive, and more feasible than experiments.

Examples

  • Is there a relationship between age and income?
  • Is there a relationship between hours of sleep and number of headaches?
  • Is there a relationship between hours of exercise per week and lifespan?

Key Features

  • Manipulation: No
  • Random Assignment: No
  • Control: No

Hypothesis Format


Null Hypothesis (H₀): There is no relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁): There is a relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.

Types of Experimental Designs

Once researchers decide to conduct an experiment, they must determine how participants will experience the different conditions of the study. Researchers must decide whether participants will experience only one condition or all conditions.

Two Common Options
  • Between-Subjects Design: Different groups experience different conditions.
  • Within-Subjects Design: The same participants experience every condition.

Between-Subjects Design

Between-Subjects Design

A between-subjects design uses separate groups of participants, where each group experiences only one condition of the independent variable.

Example

Suppose researchers want to compare two study strategies.

  • Group 1: Uses Study Strategy A
  • Group 2: Uses Study Strategy B

Each participant experiences only one study strategy.

Advantages

  • Faster completion: Participants only complete one condition.
  • No carryover effects: Previous conditions cannot influence later outcomes because participants do not experience multiple conditions.

Limitations

  • Requires more participants: Each condition requires a separate group.
  • Individual differences: Groups may differ in important characteristics before the study begins.

Within-Subjects Design

Within-Subjects Design

A within-subjects design uses the same participants in every condition of the independent variable.

Example

Suppose researchers want to compare two study strategies.

  • The same participants use Study Strategy A.
  • The same participants later use Study Strategy B.
  • Researchers compare each participant's performance across the two conditions.

Advantages

  • Requires fewer participants: The same participants complete all conditions.
  • Controls individual differences: Participants are compared with themselves.

Limitations

  • Takes longer: Participants must complete multiple conditions.
  • Carryover effects: An earlier condition may influence performance in a later condition.
