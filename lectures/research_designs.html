Research Designs

Overview

This lecture introduces the three primary research designs used in scientific research and explains how researchers select the most appropriate design to answer a research question. Before researchers can choose a research design, they must first identify the variables they want to study. A research design is built around the relationship between variables and determines how evidence will be collected to answer a research question.

Variables

Most research studies examine the relationship between two primary variables: an independent variable and a dependent variable. The independent variable is the variable that predicts or influences the outcome. The dependent variable is the outcome being measured.

Independent Variable

The independent variable is the variable that is manipulated or used to predict changes in another variable. In the hypothesis "As cybersecurity training increases, employees' ability to identify phishing emails increases," cybersecurity training is the independent variable.

Dependent Variable

The dependent variable is the outcome that is measured to determine whether it changes. In the hypothesis "As cybersecurity training increases, employees' ability to identify phishing emails increases," phishing identification accuracy is the dependent variable because it is the outcome being measured.

Additional Variables

More advanced studies may include additional variables that help explain, strengthen, or complicate relationships between the independent and dependent variables.


Mediator

A mediator explains how or why the independent variable affects the dependent variable. For example, cybersecurity training may increase employees' knowledge of phishing warning signs, which then improves their ability to identify phishing emails.


Moderator

A moderator changes the strength or direction of the relationship between the independent and dependent variable. For example, the relationship between cybersecurity training and phishing identification may be stronger for employees who regularly use email for their jobs than for employees who rarely use email.


Confounding Variable

A confounding variable is an unmeasured variable that provides an alternative explanation for the results. For example, if researchers do not measure employees' previous cybersecurity experience, it may influence their ability to identify phishing emails independently of the training. Because previous experience is not measured, researchers will not know whether differences in phishing identification are related to the training or employees' existing cybersecurity knowledge.

Research Study Designs & Key Features

Now that we understand variables, we can begin selecting the appropriate research design. Different research questions require different study designs, and each design provides different types of evidence. A research study design is the structured plan researchers use to collect evidence, measure variables, and answer a scientific research question.

There are three main research designs:true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study.

The three research designs are primarily distinguished by three scientific features: manipulation, random assignment, and control.

Manipulation

Manipulation means that the researcher created different groups for the research. For example, the researcher created a group of employees that would receive a leadership communication workshop and a group that would not receive the workshop.

Random Assignment

Random assignment means that a computer was used to randomly place participants into the different groups. For example, there were 100 employees and the computer randomly selected 50 to be in the leadership training group and the other 50 to be in the no-training group.

Control

Control means that researchers keep other factors as similar as possible so that differences in the outcome can be attributed to the independent variable. For example, employees in both groups worked in the same organization, completed the same communication assessment, and were given the same amount of time to complete it so that differences in communication scores could be attributed to the leadership workshop.

True Experiment

True Experiment

A research design with three key features: manipulation, random assignment, and control. Together, these features reduce alternative explanations and increase confidence that the independent variable caused the observed outcome. This means researchers can conclude that changes in the independent variable caused changes in the dependent variable. The results can provide strong evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship. However, experiments may not always reflect what happens in real-world settings. They also require significant time, resources, and planning. Finally, participants may behave differently because they know they are part of a study.

Example Research Scenario

Research Question Does cybersecurity training improve employees' ability to identify phishing emails?
Null Hypothesis There is no difference in phishing identification scores between employees who receive cybersecurity training and employees who do not receive the training.
Alternate Hypothesis There is a difference in phishing identification scores between employees who receive cybersecurity training and employees who do not receive the training.
Independent Variable Cybersecurity training (training or no training)
Dependent Variable Phishing identification score (percentage of phishing emails correctly identified)
Manipulation The researcher provides one group of employees with a cybersecurity training session and does not provide the other group with the training.
Random Assignment A computer randomly assigns employees to either the training group or the no-training group.
Control All employees receive the same phishing emails, testing instructions, testing environment, and amount of time to complete the test.

Quasi-Experiment

Quasi-Experiment

A quasi-experiment is a research design that includes manipulation of the independent variable but does not include random assignment. The word quasi means "almost." A quasi-experiment is an "almost experiment" because it contains some, but not all, of the features required for a true experiment. Researchers may manipulate an independent variable, but participants cannot always be randomly assigned to groups. Because the groups are not randomly created, researchers cannot completely rule out pre-existing differences between groups. This is an excellent options when groups naturally exist and cannot be randomly assigned. This is also a good option when it would be unethical to randomly assign groups to conditions. Usually less expensive and less time-consuming than a true experiment. However, the researcher cannot claim causality (that the independent variable truly caused the dependent variable.

Example Research Scenario

Research Question Does a communication workshop improve employees' workplace communication scores?
Null Hypothesis There is no difference in workplace communication scores between employees who participate in the communication workshop and employees who do not participate in the workshop.
Alternate Hypothesis There is a difference in workplace communication scores between employees who participate in the communication workshop and employees who do not participate in the workshop.
Independent Variable Communication workshop (workshop or no workshop)
Dependent Variable Workplace communication score (score on a communication assessment)
Manipulation The organization provides one working group with a communication workshop while another working group does not receive the workshop. These working groups already exist, and were not created by the researcher.
Random Assignment Employees are not randomly assigned to the workshop or no-workshop group because the employees are already members of established working groups.
Control Both working groups complete the same communication assessment under the same testing conditions and receive the same amount of time to complete it.

Correlational Study

Correlational Study

A correlational study is a research design used to measure the relationship between two or more variables without manipulating those variables. Correlational studies can identify whether variables are related, but they do not allow researchers to determine cause and effect. A correlational design is very useful for justifying future quasi-experiments or true experiments. It is also usually the cheapest and fastest research design. However, correlational designs cannot claim causality because neither variable is manipulated. A relationship between two variables does not tell us which variable causes the other, or whether a third variable explains the relationship.

Example Research Scenario

Research Question Is there a relationship between employees' weekly work hours and their job satisfaction?
Null Hypothesis There is no relationship between employees' weekly work hours and their job satisfaction.
Alternate Hypothesis There is a relationship between employees' weekly work hours and their job satisfaction.
Variable 1 Weekly work hours (number of hours employees work each week)
Variable 2 Job satisfaction (score on a job satisfaction survey)
Manipulation There is no manipulation. Researchers simply measure employees' existing work hours and job satisfaction.
Random Assignment There is no random assignment. Employees are not assigned to work a specific number of hours.
Control There is no experimental control because the researcher does not assign employees to different conditions. The researcher measures the variables as they naturally occur.

Types of Experimental Designs

Once researchers decide to conduct an experiment (a true experiment or quasi-experiment), they must determine how participants will experience the different conditions of the study. Researchers must decide whether participants will experience only one condition (between-subjects design) or all conditions (within-subjects design).

Between-Subjects Design

A between-subjects design uses separate groups of participants, where each group experiences only one condition of the independent variable.

Within-Subjects Design

A within-subjects design uses the same participants in every condition of the independent variable.

Carry Over Effect

Carryover effects are when what happens in one condition of a study affects a participant’s performance in a later condition. For example, employees may perform better on the second phishing test simply because they learned from the first test, not necessarily because of the training.

Individual Differences

Individual differences are the ways people naturally differ from one another that can affect their behavior or performance in a study. For example, if a researcher wants to train employees to identify phishing emails, some employees may already have more cybersecurity experience. They can affect employees' phishing-test scores even when everyone receives the same training.
