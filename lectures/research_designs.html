Research Design

This lecture introduces the three primary research designs used in scientific research and explains how researchers select the most appropriate design to answer a research question. Before researchers can choose an appropriate research design, they must first identify the variables they want to study. A research design is built around the relationship between variables and determines how evidence will be collected to answer a research question.

Primary Variables

Most research studies examine the relationship between two primary variables.

Variable Definition Example
Independent Variable (IV) The variable that is manipulated or used to predict changes in another variable. In the hypothesis, "As exercise increases, blood pressure decreases," exercise is the independent variable.
Dependent Variable (DV) The outcome that is measured to determine whether it changes. Blood pressure is the dependent variable because it is measured after the exercise intervention.

Additional Variables

More advanced studies may include additional variables that help explain, strengthen, or complicate relationships between the independent and dependent variables.

Variable Purpose Example
Mediator Explains how the independent variable affects the dependent variable. Exercise increases endorphins, which then reduce blood pressure.
Moderator Changes the strength or direction of the relationship. The relationship between exercise and blood pressure is stronger for cardiovascular exercise than for weightlifting.
Confounding Variable An uncontrolled variable that provides an alternative explanation for the results. Diet may influence blood pressure independently of exercise.

Research Study Designs

Now that we understand variables, we can begin selecting the appropriate research design. Different research questions require different study designs, and each design provides a different level of scientific evidence.

A research design is the overall plan researchers use to collect, measure, and analyze data to answer a research question. The design determines how participants are studied, how variables are measured, and what conclusions researchers are able to make.

Definition: Research Study Design
A research study design is the structured plan researchers use to collect evidence, measure variables, and answer a scientific research question.

Throughout this course, we will focus on the three most common research study designs:

Study Design Primary Purpose Can Researchers Claim Cause and Effect?
True Experiment Determine whether one variable causes changes in another. ✅ Yes
Quasi-Experiment Compare naturally occurring groups. ⚠ Limited
Correlational Study Determine whether two variables are related. ❌ No

The Three Features That Differentiate Research Designs

Although the three research designs look very different, they are actually distinguished by only three scientific features. As we move through this lecture, notice how each design includes different combinations of these features.

Feature Scientific Meaning Example
Manipulation The researcher deliberately changes the independent variable. Students are assigned to study for either one hour or two hours.
Random Assignment Participants are randomly placed into different conditions. A computer randomly assigns students to each study group.
Control Everything besides the independent variable is kept as similar as possible. All students receive the same instructor, materials, classroom, and exam.
Key Takeaway
The primary difference between a true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study is whether the researcher manipulates the independent variable, randomly assigns participants, and carefully controls the study. These three features determine how much confidence researchers can have in their conclusions.

True Experiment

The strongest research design because it is the only design that allows researchers to make a cause-and-effect conclusion.

Definition: True Experiment
A true experiment is a research design in which the researcher manipulates the independent variable, randomly assigns participants to conditions, and maintains a high level of control over the study.

A true experiment is the only research design that allows researchers to claim causality. Claiming causality means researchers can conclude that changes in the independent variable caused changes in the dependent variable.

The ability to make this conclusion comes from the three key features of a true experiment: manipulation, random assignment, and control. Together, these features reduce alternative explanations and increase confidence that the independent variable caused the observed outcome.

Key Features

Feature How It Appears in a True Experiment
Manipulation The researcher actively changes the independent variable to create different conditions.
Random Assignment Participants are randomly assigned to conditions, helping ensure groups are similar before the study begins.
Control The researcher keeps other factors consistent so that differences in the outcome can be attributed to the independent variable.

Example Research Question

Question: Does studying longer improve exam performance?

Research Component Example
Independent Variable Amount of study time (1 hour vs. 2 hours)
Dependent Variable Exam score percentage
Manipulation The researcher tells one group to study for 1 hour and another group to study for 2 hours.
Random Assignment A computer randomly assigns students to the 1-hour or 2-hour study condition.
Control All students receive the same study materials, instructor, classroom, and exam.

Hypothesis Format

Null Hypothesis (H₀):
There is no difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):
There is a difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Key Takeaway
A true experiment provides the strongest evidence because it includes all three scientific features: manipulation, random assignment, and control. These features allow researchers to confidently determine whether one variable causes changes in another.

Quasi-Experiment

Definition: Quasi-Experiment
A quasi-experiment is a research design that includes manipulation of the independent variable but does not include random assignment.

The word quasi means almost. A quasi-experiment is an "almost experiment" because it contains some, but not all, of the features required for a true experiment.

Researchers may manipulate an independent variable, but participants cannot always be randomly assigned to groups. Because groups are not randomly created, researchers cannot completely rule out pre-existing differences between groups.

Why Use a Quasi-Experiment?

Reason Explanation
Practical Limitations Some groups already exist naturally, making random assignment impossible.
Ethical Limitations Researchers cannot ethically assign participants to harmful or permanent conditions.
Real-World Research Many applied research questions involve naturally occurring groups that cannot be experimentally created.

Examples of Quasi-Experiments

Research Question Why It Is a Quasi-Experiment
Is there a difference in sleep hours between mothers and fathers? Participants cannot be randomly assigned to become mothers or fathers.
Is there a difference in reading level between students attending School A and School B? Students are already enrolled and cannot be randomly assigned to schools.
Is there a difference in health outcomes between people who exercise regularly and people who do not? Exercise habits occur naturally and cannot be randomly assigned long term.

Key Features

Feature Present in Quasi-Experiment?
Manipulation ✅ Yes
Random Assignment ❌ No
Control ✅ Often Yes

Hypothesis Format

Null Hypothesis (H₀):
There is no difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):
There is a difference in the outcome between Group A and Group B.

Key Takeaway
Quasi-experiments are valuable when true experiments are not possible. However, because participants are not randomly assigned, researchers must be cautious when interpreting results and cannot make the same strong causal claims as they can with a true experiment.

Correlational Study

Definition: Correlational Study
A correlational study is a research design used to measure the relationship between two or more variables without manipulating or controlling those variables.

Correlational studies are the weakest of the three research designs because they do not allow researchers to determine cause and effect.

Possible Explanations for a Correlation

Possible Explanation Description
Variable 1 Causes Variable 2 One variable may influence changes in the other variable.
Variable 2 Causes Variable 1 The direction of the relationship may be opposite of what researchers expected.
Third Variable Explanation A separate variable may explain why the two original variables are related.
Spurious Relationship The relationship may occur by coincidence and may not represent a meaningful connection.

Why Use Correlational Studies?

  • Exploration: Identify patterns and relationships for future research.
  • Prediction: Use relationships between variables to predict outcomes.
  • Practical Research: Often faster, less expensive, and more feasible than experiments.

Examples of Correlational Studies

Research Question Variables Measured
Is there a relationship between age and income? Age and income
Is there a relationship between sleep and headaches? Hours of sleep and number of headaches
Is there a relationship between exercise and lifespan? Hours of exercise per week and lifespan in years

Key Features

Feature Present in Correlational Study?
Manipulation ❌ No
Random Assignment ❌ No
Control ❌ No

Hypothesis Format

Null Hypothesis (H₀):
There is no relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.

Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):
There is a relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.

Key Takeaway
Correlational studies are useful for identifying relationships between variables, but they cannot determine whether one variable causes another.

Types of Experimental Designs

When researchers conduct experiments, they must decide how participants will experience the different conditions of the study.

Researchers must decide whether participants will experience only one condition or all conditions.

Design Description
Between-Subjects Design Different groups of participants experience different conditions.
Within-Subjects Design The same participants experience every condition.

Between-Subjects Design

Definition: Between-Subjects Design
A between-subjects design uses separate groups of participants, where each group experiences only one condition of the independent variable.
Advantage or Limitation Explanation
✅ Faster Completion Participants only complete one condition.
✅ No Carryover Effects Previous conditions cannot influence later outcomes.
❌ Requires More Participants Each condition requires a separate group.
❌ Individual Differences Groups may differ in important characteristics.

Within-Subjects Design

Definition: Within-Subjects Design
A within-subjects design uses the same participants in every condition of the independent variable.
Advantage or Limitation Explanation
✅ Requires Fewer Participants The same participants complete all conditions.
✅ Controls Individual Differences Participants are compared with themselves.
❌ Takes Longer Participants must complete multiple conditions.
❌ Carryover Effects Earlier conditions may influence later conditions.

Comparing Experimental Designs

Feature Between-Subjects Within-Subjects
Number of Groups Multiple Groups One Group
Participant Experience One Condition All Conditions
Main Strength No Carryover Effects Controls Individual Differences
Main Weakness Requires More Participants Risk of Carryover Effects
Key Takeaway
Between-subjects and within-subjects designs are two ways researchers organize experimental conditions. The best choice depends on the research question, available resources, and whether researchers are more concerned about individual differences or carryover effects.

Final Key Takeaway

Researchers must select a study design that matches their research question. True experiments provide the strongest evidence because they allow causal conclusions. Quasi-experiments provide useful evidence when random assignment is not possible. Correlational studies identify relationships between variables but cannot determine cause and effect.

Understanding research design is essential because the design determines what conclusions researchers can accurately make from their data.
