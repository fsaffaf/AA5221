Why Data Quality Must Come Before Descriptive Statistics

Learning Objectives
  • Explain why data quality should be evaluated before calculating descriptive statistics.
  • Identify common data quality problems, including missing values, duplicates, inconsistent labels, and incorrect values.
  • Describe how poor data quality can affect analytical conclusions.
  • Apply a basic data inspection process before conducting statistical analysis.
  • Recognize the importance of documenting data-cleaning decisions.

1. Introduction: Think Like an Analyst

Imagine that you work as a cybersecurity analyst, IT specialist, or organizational leader. Your manager asks you to review a dataset and report what is happening in the organization.

  • A cybersecurity analyst might examine the number of security incidents reported each month.
  • An IT analyst might examine help desk tickets, system downtime, or technical support response times.
  • An organizational leader might examine employee productivity, job satisfaction, or staff turnover.

Your manager wants answers supported by data. You might begin by calculating the average, counting records, or identifying the most common category.

However, there is an important step you must complete first: check the quality of your data.

Real-world datasets are not always clean or accurate. They may contain missing values, duplicate records, inconsistent labels, or values that were entered incorrectly.

Key Principle: Before asking what your data tells you, make sure you understand what your data contains.

2. What Is Data Quality?

Data quality refers to how suitable data is for the task you want to complete. High-quality data is sufficiently accurate, complete, consistent, and relevant for the analysis.

Consider a dataset containing cybersecurity incidents reported by employees. Before calculating the average number of incidents per department, you need to determine whether the records are complete, whether incidents have been counted more than once, and whether department names are recorded consistently.

Four Common Data Quality Problems

Problem Example Why It Matters
Missing Values An IT ticket dataset contains response times for most tickets, but some tickets have no recorded response time. Missing information can lead to inaccurate summary statistics and incomplete analysis.
Duplicate Records A cybersecurity incident is entered twice because it was submitted through multiple reporting channels. Duplicates can inflate counts and create a misleading picture of organizational activity.
Inconsistent Labels Departments are listed as "IT," "Information Technology," and "Info Tech." Software may treat identical categories as different groups.
Incorrect or Unusual Values A help desk ticket shows a response time of −25 minutes. Impossible or inaccurate values can distort statistics and conclusions.

3. How Poor Data Quality Affects Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics summarize the characteristics of a dataset. Common examples include the mean, median, mode, minimum, maximum, and frequency counts.

Although these statistics are useful, they do not automatically tell you whether the data is reliable.

Example: Ticket Resolution Times

The final ticket took 300 minutes, or five hours. The mean resolution time is 82 minutes, even though four of the five tickets took between 20 and 35 minutes.

The unusually high value of 300 pulls the mean upward and changes the overall summary.

Before using the result, you should investigate the 300-minute value. It may represent a legitimate long-duration ticket, or it could be a data-entry error. Statistics summarize all values in a dataset, including values that may be incorrect.

4. Why Data Quality Matters in Your Field

Cybersecurity

Duplicate incident reports can inflate incident counts, while missing records can make the organization appear safer than it actually is. Analysts should verify incident classifications, duplicates, and missing information before reporting trends.

Information Technology

Comparing support teams using average resolution times can be misleading if data is recorded differently across teams or if important fields are missing. Consistency matters before making comparisons.

Leadership and Organizational Decision-Making

Employee productivity studies can be distorted by duplicate employee records, inconsistent department names, or missing performance information. Decisions about staffing, budgets, and training should be based on reliable data.

Poor data quality can lead to poor decisions. Analysts must evaluate the trustworthiness of the data before drawing conclusions.

5. What Should You Check Before Calculating Statistics?

  1. Inspect column names and data types. Verify that each field contains the type of data you expect, such as numbers, text, or dates.
  2. Check for missing values. Determine where information is missing and evaluate whether it affects your analysis.
  3. Check for duplicate records. Investigate repeated rows and determine whether they are accidental duplicates or legitimate events.
  4. Inspect category labels. Look for inconsistent spelling, abbreviations, capitalization, and naming conventions.
  5. Look for unusual or impossible values. Investigate negative durations, unrealistic measurements, impossible dates, and values outside expected ranges.
  6. Document your decisions. Record what you found, what you changed, and the reasoning behind each decision.

6. Cleaning Data Requires Judgment

Data cleaning is not simply deleting anything that looks incorrect. Analysts must evaluate the meaning of the data and the purpose of the analysis before deciding how to handle issues.

Issue Possible Action
Missing Values Exclude records from a calculation, retain them for another analysis, or investigate why data is missing.
Duplicate Records Remove confirmed duplicates while retaining valid repeated events.
Inconsistent Labels Standardize labels once you confirm they represent the same category.
Unusual Values Correct verified errors or retain unusual observations if they are valid.

Analysts should never change data simply to produce more desirable results. If uncertainty remains, document it and explain how it may influence the findings.

7. Putting It All Together

Imagine your manager asks you to calculate the average number of cybersecurity incidents reported by each department. Before calculating those averages, ask:

  • Are any incident records missing important information?
  • Are some incidents recorded more than once?
  • Are department names consistent?
  • Are incident counts recorded correctly?
  • Do I understand what each row represents?
  • Have I documented any decisions I made about the data?

After inspecting the data, addressing issues you can verify, and documenting your decisions, you can proceed with descriptive statistics more confidently.

Key Takeaway

Check the data before calculating the statistics.

Descriptive statistics can reveal patterns and summarize information, but their usefulness depends on the quality of the underlying data. Analysts in cybersecurity, information technology, and leadership roles must inspect their data, investigate potential problems, make defensible decisions, and document their process before using statistics to inform decisions.
