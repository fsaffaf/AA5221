Overview

There are two types of data: quantitative and qualitative.

Quantitative data is number‑based data that allows mathematical and statistical analysis (e.g., height in centimeters, weight in kilograms).
Qualitative data is word‑based data that provides meaning and context (e.g., interviews about opinions).

While qualitative analysis focuses on meaning and experience, quantitative analysis allows precise measurement and hypothesis testing. This course focuses on quantitative data.

Descriptive vs. Inferential Statistics

Statistics fall into two main categories: descriptive and inferential.

Descriptive Statistics: Describe characteristics of a sample (mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation) and assess assumptions such as normality.
Inferential Statistics: Test hypotheses and allow conclusions about populations based on sample data (e.g., t‑tests).

Sample vs. Population

Population is the entire group of interest; a sample is a smaller subset that can realistically be studied.

Descriptive statistics describe the sample, while inferential statistics generalize findings to the population. Unrepresentative samples lead to biased conclusions.

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics are essential for understanding your data and choosing appropriate inferential tests.

  1. Understand the structure of the sample
  2. Guide inferential test selection

Mean
The mathematical average. Sensitive to extreme values.

Median
The middle value. Useful when data contain outliers.

Mode
The most frequently occurring value. Best for categorical data.

Standard Deviation (SD): Measures how spread out values are around the mean.

SD is interpreted relative to the mean (e.g., M = 10, SD = 2 → range ≈ 8–12).

Levels of Measurement

The level of measurement determines which statistics and graphs are appropriate.

  1. Nominal
  2. Ordinal
  3. Interval
  4. Ratio
Categorical Data: Nominal and ordinal
Continuous Data: Interval and ratio
