Descriptive Statistics

TYPES OF DATA

In research, data refers to the information that researchers collect in order to answer questions. The type of data you collect determines how you analyze it and what conclusions you can draw.

There are two primary types of data: quantitative and qualitative. Understanding the difference between these is essential for choosing the correct research methods. Thy type of data determines how it can be analyzed.

Quantitative Data

Quantitative data is numerical data. It involves measurements or quantities that can be analyzed mathematically using statistics.

Quantitative data answers questions like: How much? How many? How often?

Because this type of data is expressed in numbers, researchers can calculate averages, compare groups, and run statistical tests to draw conclusions.

Examples

  • Height (e.g., 170 cm)
  • Weight (e.g., 150 lbs)
  • Test scores (e.g., 85%)
  • Number of hours studied

Quantitative data is especially useful for testing hypotheses and identifying patterns or relationships between variables.

💬 Qualitative Data

Qualitative data is descriptive, non-numerical data. It focuses on understanding meaning, experience, or perspective rather than measuring quantities.

Qualitative data answers questions like: Why? How? What does it mean?

This type of data is often collected through interviews, open-ended survey responses, or observations.

Examples

  • Interview responses about opinions
  • Descriptions of experiences
  • Observations of behavior
  • Written feedback

Instead of statistical analysis, qualitative data is analyzed by identifying patterns, themes, or meanings within the responses.

TYPES OF STATISTICS

Data be divided into two main areas: descriptive statistics and inferential statistics. Each serves different purposes in research.

Descriptive Statistics Statistics that 1) describe the characteristics of a sample, using measures such as the mean, median, mode, range, and standard deviation and 2) Check whether the data meet the assumptions needed for inferential statistics (especially assumptions about normality and the shape of the data distribution)..
Inferential Statistics Inferential Statistics: Statistics that test whether the null or alternate hypotheses is supported. These statistics allow us to make conclusions about a population based on sample data. Examples are the independent t-test, dependent t-test, and Wilcoxon Sign-Rank test.

Sample vs. Population

The population is the entire group you are interested in studying. For example, if you are studying Tesla owners in the US, the population includes all Tesla owners across the country (let's say that number is 600,000).

A sample is small part of the population that you can realistically access and collect data from. For example, let's say we could only collect data from 100 Tesla owners.

Descriptive statistics focus on describing the sample (e.g., the 100 Tesla owners). They help you summarize and understand the details of your data, such as central tendencies and variability.

Inferential statistics aim to make predictions about the population (e.g., the 600,000 Tesla owners) based on the findings within your sample.

When conducting inferential statistics, the composition of your sample is critically important. The accuracy and usefulness of your conclusions depend on how well your sample represents the population you are studying.

For example, imagine your population of interest is 50% male and 50% female, but your collected sample is 80% male and 20% female. In this case, any inferences drawn from your sample may not accurately reflect the population, because the sample is biased and unrepresentative. The study of how samples are drawn and how representative they are is a branch of statistics called sampling theory. While it is a complex and deep topic, it is essential for ensuring that your inferential statistics are valid (true) and reliable (repeatable).

Descriptive Statistics (Detailed)

Even though descriptive statistics are relatively basic, they are essential. Students often rush to inferential methods, skipping descriptives, which can lead to flawed conclusions.

Always give descriptive statistics the attention they deserve.

Descriptive statistics are needed to:

  • Understand the structure and characteristics of your sample
  • Guide which inferential statistical methods are appropriate

Mean

The mean is the mathematical average of a set of numbers. It is calculated by adding all values and dividing by the number of values.

The mean is sensitive to extreme values (outliers), which can pull it upward or downward. For example, the mean exam score of a class might be 85%.

Median

The median is the midpoint of a dataset when values are arranged from lowest to highest.

If there is an odd number of values, the median is the middle number. If even, it is the average of the two middle numbers.

The median is especially useful when the mean is distorted by outliers. For example: [30k, 40k, 50k, 60k, 1M] → median = 50k.

Mode

The mode is the most frequently occurring value in a dataset.

It is particularly useful for categorical data. For example, most students reporting owning a dog in a survey.

Standard Deviation (SD)

The standard deviation (SD) measures how spread out the data is around the mean.

SD must be interpreted with the mean, not on its own.

To understand SD, calculate a range:

  • Mean + SD
  • Mean - SD

Example: If M = 10 and SD = 2 → range = 8 to 12. Reported as: (M = 10, SD = 2)

Low SD: Data points are close to the mean (e.g., exam scores 84–86%).
High SD: Data points are spread out (e.g., exam scores 75–95%).

Descriptive statistics not only summarize data but provide insight into how consistent or variable the data is.

Levels of Measurement

There are four types of data, also known as levels of measurement. Being able to correctly identify the level of your variables is essential for choosing the correct analysis.

You must identify the level of measurement to determine:

  • Which descriptive statistics can be calculated
  • Which graphs should be used
  • Which inferential test is appropriate
The four levels of measurement are: Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio

Categorical Data (qualitative): Nominal and ordinal
Continuous Data (quantitative): Interval and ratio

Nominal Data

Nominal data is categorical data with no rank order.

Example: race categories (Asian, Black, White, Hispanic, Native American). These are labels with no numerical meaning and no ranking.

  • Race
  • Gender
  • Nationality
  • State of residence

Ordinal Data

Ordinal data is categorical data with a rank order, but the distance between values is not equal.

A common example is a Likert scale (e.g., strongly disagree → strongly agree).

While responses have order, the spacing between them is not guaranteed to be equal.

  • Likert scale responses
  • Restaurant star ratings
  • Olympic medals (Gold, Silver, Bronze)

Interval Data

Interval data is numerical with equal spacing between values, but no true zero.

Example: temperature in Celsius. Zero does not represent the absence of heat, and values can go below zero.

  • Temperature (Celsius or Fahrenheit)
  • Psychological scale scores (e.g., anxiety, stress)

Ratio Data

Ratio data is numerical with equal spacing and a true zero point.

A value of zero means the complete absence of the variable, and values cannot go below zero.

  • Milligrams
  • Kilograms
  • Centimeters
  • Meters
Key Idea: Ratio data is the most informative level because it contains all properties of the other levels.

Histograms & Bar Charts

Understanding your data type determines how you should visualize it. Two of the most common graphs are histograms and bar charts.

Histograms = continuous data | Bar charts = categorical data

Histogram

  • Used for continuous data (interval and ratio)
  • Used to check normality
  • Must be created for continuous data
  • Bars touch (represent ranges/bins)
  • X-axis: value ranges (bins)
  • Y-axis: frequency

Bar Chart

  • Used for categorical data (nominal and ordinal)
  • Used to compare groups
  • Typically used when 3+ groups
  • Bars do not touch
  • X-axis: categories
  • Y-axis: value or frequency

Checking Normality with Histograms

Inferential statistics depend on whether your continuous data are normally distributed. Choosing the wrong test can lead to invalid conclusions.

Normality does NOT apply to categorical data.

Normal Distribution

A normal distribution is a theoretical pattern that many real-world variables follow (e.g., test scores, height).

  • Mean ≈ Median ≈ Mode
  • Symmetrical shape (mirror image)
  • Bell-shaped curve (most values near center)

Skewness

Skewness refers to how symmetrical a distribution is.

  • Positive skew (right skew): Tail points right, most data on left
  • Negative skew (left skew): Tail points left, most data on right

Kurtosis

Kurtosis describes the shape of the distribution:

  • Leptokurtic: Tall and narrow (too peaked)
  • Mesokurtic: Normal bell shape
  • Platykurtic: Flat and wide

Standard Deviation & Variation

Standard deviation (SD) measures how spread out data are around the mean:

  • Low SD: Data tightly clustered
  • High SD: Data widely spread out

Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7)

  • 68% of data within ±1 SD
  • 95% within ±2 SD
  • 99.7% within ±3 SD
The empirical rule helps estimate where most data points fall in a normal distribution without checking every value.

Even though not all data are perfectly normal, many continuous variables approximate normality, which allows researchers to apply inferential statistics correctly.
