Independent T-Test & Mann-Whitney U Test

Steps to Conduct an Independent t-test (or Mann-Whitney U) in RStudio

Prepare
Overview of the Test

An Independent T-Test tests if there is a difference between the scores of two different groups. When two different groups are compared, this is called a between-subjects design.

The independent t-test is extremely sensitive and requires normal data. When data is abnormal, the back-up test is the Mann-Whitney U test.

The independent t-test is preferred because it is a more statistically powerful test. This means it is more effective at detecting an effect if there is one. However, the Mann-Whitney U test is a very useful and legitimate test to use when data is abnormal.

Because normnality is so important, data analysts check normality in at least three different ways before determining which test to use.

Research Question General Question Example
Research Question Is there a mean difference between two independent groups? Is there a difference in exam scores between online students and in-person students?
Null Hypothesis There is NO difference between the two group means. There is NO difference in exam scores between online and in-person students.
Alternative Hypothesis There IS a difference between the two group means. There IS a difference in exam scores between online and in-person students.

Step 5
Normality Check 2: Boxplots

Before conducting the t-test, create boxplots to check for extreme outliers in your numerical variables.

An outlier is a score that is unusually high or low compared to the other scores in the dataset. Outliers can affect the normality of a dataset. An outlier can increase the skewness of a dataset, which can be the difference between normal and abnormal data.

A box-and-whisker plot, or boxplot, provides a quick visual summary of a numerical variable. The box shows the middle 50% of the data, the line inside the box shows the median, and the whiskers (lines outside the box) extend to the typical lower and higher values. Individual dots outside the whiskers represent potential outliers. A boxplot allows you to quickly see the center, spread, and unusual values in a continuous variable.

What Causes Outlier?

Sometimes the outlier is a legitimate datapoint. For example, let's say we were collecting data on the average income of a U.S. citizen and, somehow, Elon Musk took our survey. Although his data is legitimate, he would be an outlier that severely impacts our dataset and causes the data to be abnormal (specifically negatively skewed). Sometimes an outlier is due to a survey response error, such as a participant accidentally adding an extra zero to their annual income.

What do we do with outliers?

In more advanced data analytics classes, we investigate potential outliers before deciding what to do with them. Check the original data and consider the value in the context of the variable and research question. Ask: Is this value correct? Does it make sense? Is there a reasonable explanation for why it is unusual? If an outlier is the result of a data-entry or measurement error, it may be appropriate to correct or remove it. If the value is accurate and represents a legitimate observation, do not remove it simply because it is unusual. Any decision to remove an observation should have a clear justification and should be documented. Removing a legitimate outlier can change the distribution, sample size, and results of your analysis.

Step 6
Normality Check 3: Shapiro-Wilk

The Shapiro-Wilk test allows you to check whether your data is normal or abnormal using a statistical test. The test asks: "Is there a significant difference between my data's distribution and a theoretically perfect distribution?" A p-value of less than .05 indicates there is a significant difference, which means the data is abnormal. A p-value of greater than .05 indiciates there is no significant difference, which means the data is normal.

Code Template

shapiro.test(DatasetName$OutcomeVariable[DatasetName$GroupVariable == "Group1"])
shapiro.test(DatasetName$OutcomeVariable[DatasetName$GroupVariable == "Group2"])

Example

shapiro.test(DatasetZ$ExamScores[DatasetZ$Gender == "Female"])
shapiro.test(DatasetZ$ExamScores[DatasetZ$Gender == "Male"])

Male

p-value Interpretation Decision

p < .05 There is a significant difference between our data's distribution and a normal distribution. Data is abnormal

Degrees of Freedom: Total sample size minus the number of groups. For two groups, if n = 100, then df = 98.
