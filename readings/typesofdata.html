Types of Data

Overview

Properly identifying types of data is one of the most important skills in statistics and analytics. A variable's data type determines which statistical techniques, charts, and analyses are appropriate for your data.

Nominal

Categories with no rank order. Commonly used to collect demographic data. This data is easy to collect and analyze. Excellent for simple categorization. However, this level of data is the simplest, which heavily limits mathematical operations (i.e., types of analyses/ level of information that can be extracted from the data).

Ordinal

The most common ordinal-level measurement is a single-item Likert measure. A single-item measure asks one specific question. It is simple, efficient, quick to administer, and reduces survey fatigue. However, a single question may not fully capture a complex concept.

Interval

One of the most common interval-level measure is a multiple-item rating scale. A multiple-item measure uses several related questions or statements to assess a single variable or construct. For example, instead of measuring job satisfaction with one question, a researcher might ask participants several questions about different aspects of their job satisfaction. The responses to the individual questions are then combined into one overall score for each participant. Researchers typically calculate either a sum score, by adding the responses together, or an average score, by calculating the mean of the responses. The resulting score represents the participant's overall level of the construct being measured. Using multiple questions to measure the same construct can provide several benefits: Greater reliability:** Multiple questions can provide a more consistent measure than relying on a single question. Improved measurement precision:** Multiple items allow researchers to capture different aspects of the same construct. Reduced random error:** Random error affecting one question is less likely to have a large effect on the overall score when multiple questions are combined. More stable results:** Combining several related items can produce scores that are less affected by an unusual response to any single question. Ability to evaluate measurement quality:** Researchers can examine whether the items work well together and whether the scale consistently measures the intended construct. However, multiple-item measures also have limitations. A major drawback is **survey fatigue**, which occurs when participants become tired, bored, or less motivated as they answer a long series of questions. This can lead to incomplete responses, careless responses, or participants dropping out of the study. Another important consideration is **scale selection**. Researchers should ideally use a **pre-existing scale that has been validated through previous research** rather than creating a new scale without evidence that it works. A validated scale has been developed and tested by researchers to determine whether it reliably and accurately measures the intended construct. Using an established scale can provide evidence that the measure has acceptable **reliability and validity**. For example, if a researcher wants to measure **perceived stress**, they could use an established stress scale rather than creating several new questions and assuming that the questions accurately measure stress. Using a validated measure does not guarantee that the scale will work perfectly in every study, but it provides researchers with evidence about the quality of the measurement.

Ratio

Ratio-level measures must have a true zero. This means you cannot score lower than a zero. A negative or minus sign for ratio data may indicate direction (gained or lost), but does not indicate it's own seperate value. Most ratio-level measures are anything that can be measured using the metric or imperial system. For example, inches, feet, miles and centimeters, meters, and kilometers are all ratio-level measures. This level of measurement allows for the most advanced statistical analyses. These measures generally provide more information than nominal, ordinal, or interval measures. However, collecting exact numerical values may be more difficult than using categories or rating scales. It may require special instruments (such as physical scales, blood pressure cuffs, etc). If participants are reporting from memory, this may be more difficult for them to report than other levels of measurement because they may not know or accurately remember an exact numerical value.
