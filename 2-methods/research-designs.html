Scientific Research

Scientific Research & Research Designs

Researchers study relationships between variables every day, but not all research designs allow us to draw the same conclusions. In this lecture, we will compare three major types of research designs—true experiments, quasi-experiments, and correlational studies—and examine how manipulation, random assignment, and control influence the conclusions researchers are able to make. The definitions for all three research designs are tailored to our introductory level analytics course. More advanced definitions exist, and you will learn about them in future courses.

TRUE EXPERIMENT

A true experiment tests if there is a difference between groups. In our class, we specifically test if there is a difference between in Group A scores versus Group B scores. What distinguishes a true experiment from the other two research designs is a term called causality. A true experiment allows the researcher to "claim causality," which just means that we can say that the independent variable truly caused the dependent variable.

A true experiment must have these three key features:

Term Definition Example
Manipulation The researcher creates different groups or conditions. Comparing a high-dose medication group to a low-dose medication group.
Random Assignment Participants are randomly placed into groups. Using a random process to assign participants to treatment or control groups.
Control Everything else is kept as similar as possible between groups. Ensuring all participants complete the study in the same environment and under the same conditions.

All three research designs (true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study) are based off of different combinations of these three key features. When trying to determine if a research design is a true experiment, go through this checklist and make sure the design has all three key features:

  • ✅ Manipulation
  • ✅ Random Assignment
  • ✅ Control

Quasi-Experiment

Like a true experiment, a quasi-experiment tests if there is a difference between groups. In our class, we specifically test if there is a difference between in Group A scores versus Group B scores. A quasi-experiment is the second strongest reserach design. The prefix "quasi" means almost. A quasi-experiment is an "almost" experiment because it containes only one or two features required for a true experiment. A quasi-experiment does NOT allow the researcher to claim causality.

However, the quasi-experiment is still an important and useful research design. In fact, there are times when a researcher will intentionally choose a quasi-experiment over a true experiment. Typically, this is becasue the researcher cannot always randomly assign participants to groups. There are many scenarios where the researcher cannot assign participants to groups due to nature or ethical reasons. For example, it is not naturally possible to randomly assign participants to be born male or female. Additionally, it is not ethically possible to randomly assign participants to have a traumatic brain injury or not.

Below is one of the most common examples of a quasi-experiment:

  • ✅ Manipulation
  • ❌ Random Assignment
  • ✅ Control

Be ware that these are not the only examples of quasi-experiments. There are several others, but it would take up uneccessary space and time to show you all possible combinations.

Correlational Study

Correlational studies examine whether two variables are related, but they do have any of the key features of a true experiment.

  • ❌ Manipulation
  • ❌ Random Assignment
  • ❌ Control
Important: Correlation ≠ Causation

Even if two variables are strongly related, we cannot assume that one causes the other. There may be a third variable, or the relationship could be coincidental.

Despite being the weakest design, correlational studies are useful because they are fast, inexpensive, and great for early exploration.
