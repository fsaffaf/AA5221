Lecture 1

Scientific Research & Research Designs

OVERVIEW

Researchers study relationships between variables every day, but not all research designs allow us to draw the same conclusions. In this lecture, we will compare three major types of research designs—true experiments, quasi-experiments, and correlational studies—and examine how manipulation, random assignment, and control influence the conclusions researchers are able to make. The definitions for all three research designs are tailored to our introductory level analytics course. More advanced definitions exist, and you will learn about them in future courses.

Independent Variable: The variable manipulated by the researcher. It causes changes in the outcome variable. It is often abbreviated as IV.
Dependent Variable: The variable that is measured. It is affected by the independent variable. It is often abbreviated as DV.

TRUE EXPERIMENT

A true experiment is the only research design that allows us to claim causality. To "claim causality" just means that we can say that the independent variable (IV) truly caused the dependent variable (DV). The other two research designs cannot make this claim.

All three research designs (true experiment, quasi-experiment, and correlational study) are based off of different combinations of three key features.

Term Definition Example
Manipulation The researcher creates different groups or conditions. Comparing a high-dose medication group to a low-dose medication group.
Random Assignment Participants are randomly placed into groups. Using a random process to assign participants to treatment or control groups.
Control Everything else is kept as similar as possible between groups. Ensuring all participants complete the study in the same environment and under the same conditions.

A true experiment is the only research design which has all three features. These three features allow the researcher to remove as much bias as possible from the research, so that they can conflidently say the IV truly caused the DV.

True Experiment Hypotheses

  • Null: There is no difference in the scores between Group A and Group B.
  • Alternate: There is a difference in the scores between Group A and Group B.

Quasi-Experiment

A quasi-experiment is the second strongest reserach design. The prefix "quasi" means almost. A quasi-experiment is an "almost" experiment because it containes only one or two features required for a true experiment. A quasi-experiment does NOT allow the researcher to claim causality.

However, the quasi-experiment is still an important and useful research design. In fact, there are times when a researcher will intentionally choose a quasi-experiment over a true experiment. Typically, this is becasue the researcher cannot always randomly assign participants to groups. There are many scenarios where the researcher cannot assign participants to groups due to nature or ethical reasons. For example, it is not naturally possible to randomly assign participants to be born male or female. Additionally, it is not ethically possible to randomly assign participants to have a traumatic brain injury or not.

Correlational Study

Correlational studies are the weakest design. Just because two variables are related, we cannot assume that one causes the other. There may be a third variable, or the relationship could be coincidental. Because of this, there is technically no "true" independent variable or dependent variable in a correlational study. Often times they are simply referred to as "variables." Despite being the weakest design, correlational studies are useful because they are fast, inexpensive, and great for early exploration.

❌ Random Assignment
❌ Control

Correlational Hypotheses

  • Null: There is no relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.
  • Alternate: There is a relationship between Variable 1 and Variable 2.
