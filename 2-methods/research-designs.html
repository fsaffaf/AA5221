Scientific Research

Research Designs

In scientific research, one of the primary goals is to understand whether one variable influences another. However, not all research designs allow us to make the same level of conclusion. Some designs allow us to make strong claims about cause and effect, while others are limited to identifying relationships between variables.

You can think of research designs as existing on a spectrum from strongest to weakest: true experiments, followed by quasi-experiments, and finally correlational studies.

STRONGEST True Experiment

A true experiment is considered the strongest type of research design because it allows researchers to make a claim about causality. In other words, a researcher can confidently argue that changes in the independent variable caused changes in the dependent variable.

In order to make this claim, a true experiment must include three essential features. First, the researcher must manipulate the independent variable by creating different conditions or groups. Second, participants must be randomly assigned to these groups so that each person has an equal chance of being placed in any condition. Finally, the researcher must maintain a high level of experimental control, meaning that all other factors are kept as similar as possible between the groups.

For example, a researcher studying headache medication might assign one group to receive a high dose and another group to receive a low dose. Participants would be randomly assigned to each group, and the researcher would ensure that all other conditions—such as environment, timing, and method of administration—remain consistent.

MEDIUM Quasi-Experiment

A quasi-experiment is similar to a true experiment but does not include all three required elements. In most cases, quasi-experiments are missing random assignment. Because of this limitation, researchers cannot confidently claim that one variable caused changes in another.

Quasi-experiments are often used when true experiments are not possible due to ethical or practical reasons. For example, it would be unethical to randomly assign individuals to harmful conditions, such as exposure to dangerous substances. Instead, researchers must study groups that already exist.

Although quasi-experiments are weaker than true experiments, they still provide valuable information and are often the best option in real-world research settings.

LOW Correlational Study

Correlational studies are used to examine whether two variables are related to one another. However, unlike experiments, these studies do not involve manipulating variables or controlling conditions.

It is important to remember that correlation does not imply causation.

Even if two variables are strongly related, we cannot conclude that one causes the other. There may be other factors involved, or the relationship may be coincidental. Despite this limitation, correlational studies are useful because they are often quick, inexpensive, and helpful for identifying patterns that can be explored further in more rigorous research designs.

Between-Subjects and Within-Subjects Designs

When researchers conduct experiments, they must decide how participants will experience the different conditions of the study. This decision leads to two common designs: between-subjects designs and within-subjects designs.

Between-Subjects Design

In a between-subjects design, each participant experiences only one condition.

This means that different groups of participants are assigned to each condition. For example, one group might receive Medication A, while a separate group receives Medication B. Each participant contributes data for only one condition.

This design allows researchers to complete studies more quickly and avoids carryover effects, since participants are not exposed to multiple conditions. However, it also requires more participants, and researchers must ensure that the groups are comparable to one another.

Within-Subjects Design

In a within-subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions.

In this approach, the same participants go through every condition in the study. For example, participants might first take Medication A and later take Medication B. This allows researchers to directly compare how the same individual responds to each condition.

One advantage of this design is that it requires fewer participants and reduces differences between individuals. However, it often takes more time to complete and introduces the possibility of carryover effects, where earlier conditions influence later results.

Researchers must carefully consider these trade-offs when choosing between these two designs.
