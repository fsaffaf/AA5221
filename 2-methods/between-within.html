Lecture 2

Between vs Within Designs

📊 Understanding Experimental Design Choices

When researchers conduct experiments, they must decide how participants will experience the conditions of the study. This choice has a major impact on how the data is collected, how long the study takes, and how confident researchers can be in their results.

The two most common approaches are between-subjects designs and within-subjects designs. Each has advantages and disadvantages, and the decision depends on the goals and constraints of the study.

The key question is: Do participants experience one condition or multiple conditions?

🧍 Between-Subjects Design

In a between-subjects design, each participant is assigned to only one condition of the experiment. This means that different groups of people are used for each condition being tested.

Each participant provides data for only one condition.

For example, if a researcher is comparing two medications, one group of participants would receive Medication A, while a completely separate group would receive Medication B. Each participant only experiences one version of the treatment.

One major advantage of this design is that it avoids carryover effects, which occur when one condition influences performance in another. Because participants do not experience multiple conditions, there is no risk that earlier experiences will affect later results.

However, this design requires more participants because each condition needs its own group. Additionally, researchers must ensure that the groups are comparable, since differences between groups (such as age or health) could influence the results.

Key takeaway: Between-subjects designs are simpler and faster, but require more participants and careful group matching.

🔁 Within-Subjects Design

In a within-subjects design, the same participants experience every condition of the experiment. This allows researchers to compare how each individual responds across different conditions.

Each participant provides data for all conditions.

For example, a participant might first take Medication A and later take Medication B. Because the same person completes both conditions, the researcher can directly compare their responses.

One key advantage of this design is efficiency. Because each participant contributes data to every condition, fewer participants are needed. This also reduces variability, since individual differences are controlled automatically.

However, within-subjects designs take longer to complete and introduce the risk of carryover effects. For instance, the effects of one treatment may still be present when the participant moves to the next condition.

Researchers often use strategies such as counterbalancing, which involves varying the order of conditions, to reduce these effects.

Key takeaway: Within-subjects designs are efficient and powerful, but require strategies to manage carryover effects.

⚖️ Comparing the Designs

Both designs are widely used in research, and neither is inherently better than the other. Instead, the best choice depends on the specific goals of the study and practical considerations such as time, cost, and participant availability.

Between-Subjects Within-Subjects
Different participants in each condition Same participants in all conditions
Faster to complete Takes more time
No carryover effects Carryover effects possible
Requires more participants Requires fewer participants
Final takeaway: The choice between designs depends on whether you prioritize speed and simplicity or efficiency and precision.
